Logistics & Warehousing

A Warehouse Becomes a Financeable Asset When Location, Occupancy and Rental Cash Flow Come Together.

Logistics and warehousing assets consume capital long before they generate stable rental income. Land must be aggregated. Approvals obtained. Infrastructure developed. Buildings constructed. Tenants secured. The financing challenge changes at every stage.

EquiFin helps logistics and warehousing businesses evaluate capital requirements across development, construction, tenant stabilisation and long-term lease-backed financing.

A Warehouse Is Not Financed Only as a Building

A logistics asset may be physically simple — land, civil structure, loading infrastructure, internal roads and utilities. But institutional financing depends on much more. A lender or investor may evaluate: where is the asset located? Who will occupy it? How long will they stay? What rental income will the asset generate?

Land → Approvals → Development → Construction → Pre-Leasing → Occupancy → Rental Cash Flow → Long-Term Financing

In logistics real estate, the physical asset creates potential value. Contracted occupancy converts that potential into financeable cash flow.

The Logistics & Warehousing Capital Landscape

The Source of Capital Changes as the Asset Matures

A logistics park does not carry the same risk throughout its lifecycle. Early-stage capital may be exposed to land aggregation, approval risk and development uncertainty. Construction capital is exposed to execution, cost and completion. Operational capital is evaluated more heavily around occupancy, tenant quality and rental cash flow — a transition from private equity or private credit for land aggregation, to project debt for construction, and subsequently long-term LRD upon tenant occupancy.

Where Logistics & Warehousing Businesses Typically Need Capital

Capital requirements arise at specific asset and occupancy milestones.

01

Land Aggregation

A logistics development often begins with land. But land acquisition can involve multiple parcels, multiple sellers, title diligence and aggregation complexity — fragmented land acquisition is a specific sector financing challenge. The financing question is: can early-stage capital be deployed before the asset has operational cash flow or tenant-backed value?

02

Land Use and Development Approvals

Before institutional construction financing, the project may need regulatory clarity — delays in non-agricultural zoning conversions are a common financing friction in warehousing development. Capital providers may require comfort around land use, development approvals and master layout. An attractive location does not automatically create a financeable development site.

03

Grade-A Logistics Park Development

Capital may be required for internal roads, drainage, utilities, loading infrastructure and warehouse structures. The challenge is that significant capital may be deployed before rental income begins.

04

Built-to-Suit Warehouse Development

A BTS asset is developed around the requirements of a specific occupier. Potential financing strength may come from an identified tenant, defined facility requirement and lease visibility. But concentration can also increase — a single strong tenant can improve revenue visibility while simultaneously increasing dependence on one counterparty.

05

Tenant Fit-Out and Asset Readiness

A warehouse may require additional expenditure to become operational for a particular tenant. Capital planning must therefore consider base building cost, tenant-specific requirements and timing of tenant occupancy.

06

Operational Asset Refinancing

Once a warehouse becomes occupied and rental collections stabilise, the financing profile can change. The question shifts from "can this asset be developed?" to "how much long-term debt can the contracted rental cash flow support?"

07

Portfolio Expansion

A logistics developer operating multiple assets may need to evaluate project-level debt, portfolio-level leverage, cross-collateralisation, existing rental cash flows and new development capital.

The Core Logistics Financing Challenge

Construction Creates an Asset. Occupancy Creates a Cash-Flow Story.

A completed warehouse without tenants may have asset value. But long-term debt capacity is materially different from an occupied warehouse with contractual rentals.

Location → Asset Development → Tenant Demand → Lease Execution → Occupancy → Rental Collection → Debt Servicing Capacity

This is the central financial transformation within the sector.

EquiFin Perspective

The transition from development finance to lease-backed finance is fundamentally a transition from asset risk to cash-flow underwriting.

How Capital Providers Underwrite Logistics & Warehousing

Warehousing finance generally combines three perspectives: location quality (will the location remain commercially relevant?), tenant quality (how reliable is the counterparty paying the rent?) and lease cash-flow visibility (how long and how predictably can rental income support debt?). Freight corridor and highway connectivity, tenant creditworthiness and lease terms including lock-in periods and rent escalations are core underwriting considerations.

1. Location and Connectivity

A warehouse location is not evaluated only by geography. Capital providers may assess connectivity to freight corridors, highways, consumption centres and industrial clusters. The question is: does the location support long-term tenant demand? An asset can be physically well constructed but commercially weaker if connectivity changes or the market shifts.

2. Tenant Creditworthiness

Tenant profiles such as 3PL operators, FMCG companies and e-commerce businesses are relevant underwriting considerations. The lender may evaluate financial strength, business profile, payment history and corporate standing. Key question: how reliable is the entity responsible for the contracted rental cash flow?

3. Lease Tenure

Long-term debt needs visibility over future cash flow. The remaining lease tenure therefore becomes important — remaining lease period, lock-in period, renewal provisions and termination rights. WALE relative to loan tenure is a core logistics financing metric.

4. Rent Escalation

Rental escalation provisions can influence future cash flow. The lender evaluates whether rent remains stable and whether escalation is contractually defined, to understand how future rental income behaves over the financing period.

5. Occupancy

Occupancy is a direct indicator of whether the asset is generating income. But headline occupancy alone may not be sufficient. A lender may also ask: how concentrated is occupancy? How long are leases remaining? What happens when lock-ins expire? 100% occupancy today does not automatically mean stable occupancy over the life of the loan.

Signature Insight

Occupancy Is Not the Same as Income Stability

A Full Warehouse Can Still Carry Future Vacancy Risk

Consider two assets.

Asset A

100% occupied by multiple tenants with staggered lease expiries.

Asset B

100% occupied by one tenant with a short remaining lease.

Both have identical current occupancy. Their financing profile may be very different. This is why occupancy must be evaluated alongside tenant quality, lease tenure, WALE and concentration.

WALE: How Long Does the Rental Visibility Last?

Weighted Average Lease Expiry Is a Cash-Flow Visibility Metric

WALE provides an indication of the weighted average remaining lease term across tenants.

Longer WALE → Greater Rental Visibility → More Predictable Cash Flow → Stronger Long-Term Debt Case

WALE expectations are typically evaluated relative to proposed loan tenure, including an indicative relationship where WALE should exceed or meaningfully support the financing period.

The Core Question

Will the contracted lease cash flow remain visible for long enough to support the proposed debt?

The Lease-to-Debt Conversion

Rental Income Is the Starting Point. Net Cash Flow Determines Debt Capacity.

A simplified cash-flow structure:

Contracted Gross Rentals → Less: Operating Expenses → Net Operating Income → Debt Service → Residual Cash Flow

The lender therefore evaluates more than gross rent — maintenance costs, property expenses and unrecoverable outgoings all matter.

EquiFin Perspective

The financeable value of a warehouse is not simply determined by its area. It is determined by the durability of the net cash flow generated from that area.

Signature Insight

DSCR in Lease-Backed Warehouse Financing

Can Escrowed Rentals Service the Proposed Debt?

DSCR compares available cash flow with scheduled debt obligations.

DSCR = Cash Available for Debt Service ÷ (Interest + Principal Repayment)

An indicative LRD Escrow DSCR of approximately 1.25x or above is a common logistics and warehousing underwriting benchmark. The objective is not merely to establish whether rent covers EMI. It is to understand: how much cushion exists if the asset experiences operational or leasing pressure?

LTV: Asset Value Still Matters

Rental Cash Flow Drives Debt Capacity. Asset Value Provides Additional Credit Protection.

Lenders may also evaluate Loan-to-Value. Indicative LTV ranges of approximately 65%–70% apply for logistics and warehousing transactions, depending on the asset and transaction structure.

Asset Value → Proposed Debt → Collateral Coverage

However, a high-value warehouse does not necessarily support high leverage if the rental cash flow is weak. Both asset value and cash flow matter.

Pre-Leasing and Financing

Pre-Leasing Can Change the Development Financing Conversation

A warehouse under construction carries uncertainty. The developer must demonstrate location attractiveness, tenant demand and commercial viability. Pre-leasing can provide greater visibility. But tenant pre-leasing risk is a specific sector challenge. The strength of a pre-leasing arrangement depends on tenant quality, lease terms, execution certainty and occupancy timing.

Lender Perspective

A signed tenant relationship can improve the financing case, but the lender must still understand whether the agreement will convert into sustained cash flow after construction.

Built-to-Suit Financing

BTS Assets Are Built Around Revenue Visibility — but Also Tenant Concentration

Built-to-suit warehouses can create a clearer commercial case because the asset is designed for a specific occupier.

Potential Strengths

Defined demand, tenant alignment, lease visibility.

Potential Risks

Single-tenant concentration, specialised asset design, re-leasing challenges.

The underwriting question becomes: if the original tenant leaves, how easily can the asset be repositioned for another occupier?

Location Risk: Today's Logistics Hub May Not Always Remain Tomorrow's

Connectivity Is Part of the Credit Case

Shifting regional logistics hubs and infrastructure connectivity delays are recognised sector risks. A logistics asset can be affected by changes in freight routes, highway connectivity, industrial development and distribution patterns. This creates a longer-term question: will the location remain strategically relevant over the debt tenure?

Vacancy Risk

Lease Expiry Can Become a Financing Event

The strongest warehouse may experience periods of vacancy. Potential triggers include tenant relocation, business contraction, lease expiry and changing distribution networks — tenant vacancy following lock-in expiry is a recognised sector risk. The impact can be direct:

Tenant Exit → Vacancy → Lower Rental Income → Reduced DSCR → Debt Servicing Pressure

This is why lenders focus on both current occupancy and future lease expiry.

Tenant Concentration

One Strong Tenant Can Improve the Credit Case — and Create a Dependency

A single institutional tenant may provide strong rental visibility and good credit quality. But concentration introduces another question: what happens if that one tenant exits? A diversified tenant mix can reduce dependency but may create multiple lease expiries and management complexity. There is no universally superior structure — the financing analysis must understand the concentration profile.

Construction Finance vs Long-Term LRD

The Same Asset Can Require Two Very Different Financing Structures

During Construction

The lender focuses on development approvals, project cost, construction timeline and completion.

After Occupancy

The lender focuses more on tenant quality, rental cash flow, lease tenure and DSCR.

Development Stage (Construction Risk, Completion Risk, Cost Risk) → Stabilised Stage (Occupancy Risk, Tenant Risk, Lease Risk, Cash Flow Risk)

EquiFin Perspective

The transition from construction finance to LRD should not be treated as a simple refinancing exercise. It is a transition between two different underwriting frameworks.

Escrow and Rental Cash-Flow Control

Lease-Backed Financing Requires Visibility Over Rental Collections

Direct escrow of lease rentals and tripartite structures with tenants are potential risk-mitigation mechanisms. A simplified structure:

  1. Tenant
  2. Lease Rental
  3. Escrow Account
  4. Operating Expenses
  5. Debt Service
  6. Surplus Distribution

The purpose is to establish clarity over the cash flow supporting the debt. The lender is not only financing the building. It is underwriting the stream of rentals expected to flow through it.

Yield on Cost and Capital Efficiency

Development Economics Matter Before the Asset Becomes Operational

For a developer, the financial question begins before rental income. Total project cost may include land, development, construction and infrastructure. The resulting rental income can then be assessed relative to total cost — yield on cost and capitalisation rate are relevant financial considerations for the sector.

The broader strategic question is: does the asset being developed justify the capital being invested into it?

Capitalisation Rate and Asset Value

Long-Term Asset Value and Rental Yield Are Connected

A stabilised logistics asset may be evaluated using income-based valuation approaches.

Net Operating Income → Market Yield / Capitalisation Rate → Indicative Asset Value

Changes in rental income, occupancy and market yields can affect asset value. This matters because long-term financing may be evaluated against both cash-flow capacity and collateral value.

Refinancing Operational Warehouse Assets

A Stabilised Asset May Deserve a Different Capital Structure

An asset that has completed construction, achieved occupancy and established rental collections may have a different financing profile from the original development. Potential objectives of refinancing may include replacing shorter-term capital, aligning debt tenure with lease cash flows, consolidating facilities and supporting portfolio expansion — operational facility refinancing is a core logistics and warehousing transaction scenario.

Portfolio Financing

Multiple Warehouses Create a Different Capital Structure Question

A portfolio may include stabilised assets, newly completed assets and under-construction assets. Management must evaluate whether existing cash flows can support new development, whether cross-collateralisation is appropriate, and whether debt should remain asset-specific.

The strategic question becomes:

How should mature rental assets support future growth without exposing stable cash flows to excessive development risk?

Important Logistics & Warehousing Underwriting Metrics

Metrics help translate property performance into debt capacity.

Occupancy

Indicates the proportion of leasable space currently occupied. But must be analysed alongside tenant concentration and lease expiry.

WALE

Provides visibility into the weighted average remaining lease period.

DSCR

Measures whether available rental cash flow can support debt obligations. An indicative LRD DSCR benchmark of approximately 1.25x or above applies.

LTV

Measures proposed debt relative to asset value. Indicative logistics asset LTV ranges are around 65%–70%, subject to transaction-specific factors.

Yield on Cost

Compares the expected operational yield with the total capital invested into development.

Capitalisation Rate

Used as part of income-based asset valuation analysis.

Important Principle — no single metric determines warehouse financing capacity. Location, tenant quality, lease visibility, cash flow and collateral strength must work together.

Frequently Asked Questions

How are logistics and warehousing projects typically financed in India?

Capital requirements can vary by asset stage. Early development may require land-focused capital, construction may be supported through project-oriented debt, while stabilised and occupied assets may be evaluated for long-term lease-backed financing structures.

What do lenders evaluate before financing a warehouse?

Lenders may evaluate location connectivity, land and approval status, construction requirements, tenant quality, occupancy, lease tenure, rent escalation, WALE, DSCR and asset value.

Why is tenant quality important in warehouse financing?

For lease-backed financing, tenant rentals form the basis of expected debt servicing. The financial strength and reliability of the tenant can therefore materially influence the financing assessment.

What is WALE?

WALE, or Weighted Average Lease Expiry, indicates the weighted average remaining lease duration across an asset's tenant base. It helps assess the visibility of future contracted rental cash flows.

Can an operational warehouse be refinanced?

Refinancing may become relevant after an asset achieves occupancy and establishes rental collections. At this stage, the financing assessment can shift from development and construction risk toward lease-backed cash-flow capacity.

What is the difference between warehouse construction finance and LRD?

Construction finance focuses primarily on completing the asset and managing development risk. Lease Rental Discounting evaluates the ability of contracted rental cash flows from an occupied asset to support long-term debt.

What are the major risks in logistics and warehousing financing?

Key risks may include fragmented land acquisition, approval delays, construction delays, tenant vacancy, tenant concentration, lease expiry, connectivity challenges and changes in regional logistics patterns.
