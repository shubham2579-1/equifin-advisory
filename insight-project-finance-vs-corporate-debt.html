When Does a Business Need Project Finance Instead of Corporate Debt?

Not every large capital expenditure should be financed from the corporate balance sheet. The right financing approach depends on where repayment capacity sits, how risks are allocated and whether the underlying project can support a distinct financing structure.

10 Min Read·Perspective·September 2026

The Question

A company plans a significant new investment — a renewable energy project, a road or infrastructure asset, a manufacturing facility, a logistics development, a hospitality asset, a data centre or a specialised industrial project. The management team has identified the opportunity: land may be available, approvals may be progressing, contracts may be under discussion, and capital expenditure has been estimated. The next question is financing. A common approach is to ask whether the company can raise a term loan for this project. But before approaching the market, there is another question worth asking: should this investment be financed as part of the corporate balance sheet, or should the project itself form the basis of the financing structure?

This distinction can materially change how lenders evaluate the transaction, what information is required, how leverage is assessed, how cash flows are analysed, what security may be required, how repayment is structured and which institutions may be relevant. Corporate debt and project finance both provide capital, but they do not necessarily analyse risk in the same way.

The Fundamental Difference

At a simplified level, the distinction can be understood through the source of repayment. Under corporate debt, the lender primarily evaluates the broader borrowing company — corporate financial performance, existing cash flows, balance sheet strength, existing leverage, management track record, asset base and overall repayment capacity. The proposed investment may be important, but the corporate balance sheet remains central to the credit evaluation. Under project finance, the focus shifts more directly towards the economics and cash flows of the underlying project — project cost, implementation timeline, contract structure, revenue visibility, operating assumptions, projected cash flows, risk allocation and debt servicing capacity. The project becomes a central unit of analysis. In practice, financing structures can sit across a spectrum rather than within two completely separate categories, but understanding this fundamental distinction is important.

Project Finance Is Not Simply “Large Debt”

One common misconception is that project finance is simply financing for a large project. Size alone does not define the financing approach — a large investment may still be financed through conventional corporate borrowing, while a smaller project may be evaluated through a dedicated project structure because its cash flows, contracts and risk allocation can be separately assessed. The more relevant question is whether the underlying project can be analysed as a distinct economic and cash-flow proposition. For a project-oriented financing approach to be meaningful, the transaction generally needs sufficient clarity around capital expenditure, development timeline, revenue generation, operating assumptions, cash-flow visibility and risk allocation. The financing structure is therefore linked to the underlying economics of the project—not merely the amount of capital required.

Corporate debt and project finance do not necessarily analyse risk the same way. The distinction can be summarised as two different paths from the borrowing entity to debt repayment.

01 — Where Does Repayment Capacity Sit?

This is often the starting point. Suppose an established company is undertaking an expansion and the corporate entity already generates significant operating cash flows — the proposed expansion is one part of a larger business, and a lender may be comfortable evaluating the financing against the company’s overall financial profile, with repayment capacity linked to the broader enterprise. Now consider a dedicated project being developed, where significant capital must be deployed before operations begin and, once operational, the asset is expected to generate defined cash flows over an extended period. Here, the project’s economics may become central to the financing analysis — the lender may ask what cash flows this specific project will generate, and whether those cash flows can support the proposed debt. This difference can shape the entire transaction.

02 — The Project Must Have an Identifiable Economic Framework

Project-oriented financing requires more than a business plan — the underlying project needs a sufficiently clear economic structure. Depending on the sector, this may involve understanding the total project cost, how long development will take, what will generate income, the ongoing operating expenses, whether revenues or key costs are supported by contractual arrangements, how long the asset will generate economic value, and when the project will begin producing cash. The objective is to understand the complete economic lifecycle, from development and construction through commissioning, operations, revenue generation, operating cash flow and debt servicing. A project with limited clarity around these stages may be more difficult to evaluate through a dedicated project financing framework.

03 — Construction Risk Changes the Financing Conversation

Many projects do not begin generating cash immediately — there may be a significant gap between capital deployment and revenue generation. During this period, multiple risks may exist, including construction delays, cost overruns, approval delays, contractor performance issues, supply chain disruption and changes in input costs. From a financing perspective, the question becomes what happens if the project takes longer or costs more than originally expected. This is why project financing often requires significant attention to implementation planning — who is executing the project, what the implementation schedule is, what contingencies exist, how cost overruns will be funded, whether critical approvals are in place, and what dependencies remain. The project does not become financeable simply because projected returns appear attractive; the pathway from construction to operations also matters.

04 — Revenue Visibility Can Be Critical

Once the project is operational, debt servicing depends on the ability to generate cash, so lenders may seek to understand the visibility and reliability of future revenues. The analysis differs by sector — for renewable energy, potential considerations may include power purchase arrangements, counterparty profile, generation assumptions and the tariff framework; for infrastructure, concession terms, traffic or utilisation assumptions, payment mechanisms and contractual obligations; for hospitality, occupancy assumptions, average room rates, market demand and seasonality; for industrial projects, capacity utilisation, customer demand, supply arrangements and margin assumptions. The key question remains consistent: what supports the project’s ability to generate predictable cash flows?

05 — Project Finance Requires Thinking About Risk Allocation

Every major project involves risks — land risk, approval risk, construction risk, cost overrun risk, operating risk, demand or revenue risk and counterparty risk. The objective is not to pretend those risks do not exist; the more important question is who bears each risk. For each major risk, the transaction should ideally identify who is responsible, what contractual protection exists, how the risk is mitigated, and what happens if the risk materialises. This is one reason project financing can require a more detailed transaction framework — the lender is not only evaluating a borrower, it is evaluating how the project will move from concept to cash generation.

06 — The Role of Special Purpose Vehicles

Certain projects are undertaken through a dedicated Special Purpose Vehicle (SPV), created to establish a distinct legal and financial structure around the project — separating project assets, project liabilities, project contracts and project cash flows, which can make it easier to evaluate the project as a distinct transaction. However, the existence of an SPV alone does not automatically make something project finance. The underlying project still needs a credible economic and financial framework, supported by defined assets, identifiable capital costs, contractual arrangements, forecastable operations and analysable cash flows. The legal structure and financial structure need to work together.

Corporate Balance Sheet Strength Still Matters

Project finance does not necessarily mean the corporate sponsor becomes irrelevant — promoter and sponsor strength can remain important. Lenders may still consider sponsor experience, execution capability, financial strength, equity contribution and track record in the sector. During the development stage especially, project cash flows may not yet exist, so institutional comfort may depend partly on the ability of the sponsors to support the project through construction and implementation. The practical transaction may involve both corporate support and project cash flows — the balance depends on the nature and maturity of the project.

07 — Equity Contribution Is Part of the Project Capital Structure

Project financing is rarely about debt in isolation. The capital structure may include a combination of sponsor equity, senior debt, subordinated capital and other forms of structured capital where relevant. The relationship between debt and sponsor contribution can influence how the transaction is perceived — a meaningful sponsor contribution can demonstrate alignment with the project’s economics and risks. The objective is not to apply a universal debt-equity ratio; different sectors and projects have different risk profiles. The more relevant consideration is whether the overall capital structure provides sufficient resilience for the project to be developed and operated successfully.

08 — Cash Flow Waterfall Becomes Important

In project-oriented financing, the movement of cash can become a central structural consideration — project revenue flowing through operating expenses, statutory obligations, interest payment, principal repayment, reserves and other requirements, before arriving at surplus cash flow. The precise waterfall will differ across transactions, but the concept is important: the lender wants to understand how project cash flows move through the financial structure and whether sufficient cash remains available for debt obligations. This is fundamentally different from simply looking at consolidated corporate profitability.

09 — Project Finance Requires a Different Financial Model

A standard corporate financial model may focus on revenue growth, EBITDA, profitability and balance sheet trends. A project financing model generally needs to go deeper into the project’s lifecycle — project cost, funding schedule and construction timeline during the development phase; revenue assumptions, operating costs and maintenance requirements during the operating phase; interest during construction, debt drawdowns and repayment schedule in the financing phase; and operating cash generation, debt servicing and sensitivity analysis in the cash-flow analysis. The model is not simply forecasting business performance — it is testing whether the project economics can support the proposed capital structure.

EquiFin Perspective

The choice between corporate debt and project finance should not begin with the financing product. It should begin with understanding where the economic risk sits and where the repayment capacity will come from.

Key Takeaways

  • Project finance is not defined simply by the size of the capital expenditure.
  • The key distinction often lies in where the primary repayment capacity is expected to come from.
  • Corporate debt focuses primarily on the broader company’s financial strength and cash flows.
  • Project-oriented financing places greater emphasis on project economics, implementation and future project cash flows.
  • Construction, approval and cost-overrun risks can materially influence financing discussions.
  • Revenue visibility and contractual structures can be important to evaluating project repayment capacity.
  • Project financing requires detailed analysis of the complete lifecycle—from development through operations and debt servicing.
  • The right financing approach should follow the economic characteristics of the transaction rather than the size of the funding requirement alone.

Closing Perspective

A major capital investment can be transformational for a business. But before deciding how to finance it, management should understand what the capital is actually supporting — is the business borrowing against the strength of its existing enterprise, or is it developing an asset with its own identifiable lifecycle, risks and future cash flows? The answer influences much more than the financing source. It shapes the entire transaction.

The most effective approach is therefore not to begin with which financing product to use. It is to begin with the economic structure of the investment, where repayment capacity sits, and what financing framework best reflects that reality. That is where project financing begins.
