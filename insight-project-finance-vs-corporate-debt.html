When Does a Business Need Project Finance Instead of Corporate Debt?

Not every large capital expenditure should be financed from the corporate balance sheet. The right financing approach depends on where repayment capacity sits, how risks are allocated and whether the underlying project can support a distinct financing structure.

7 Min Read·Perspective·September 2026

The Question

A company plans a major investment — a renewable energy project, a road, a manufacturing facility, a data centre. Land may be available, approvals progressing, capex estimated. The natural next question is financing — can we get a term loan for this? There's a prior question worth asking first: should this be financed against the corporate balance sheet, or should the project itself be the basis of the financing structure?

The distinction changes how lenders evaluate the transaction, what information is required, and which institutions are even relevant. Under corporate debt, the lender evaluates the broader company — its cash flows, balance sheet, existing leverage, track record. Under project finance, the focus shifts to the project itself — its cost, contracts, revenue visibility and cash flows become the central unit of analysis. Size alone doesn't decide which applies: a large investment can still be financed conventionally, while a smaller project with clean, separable cash flows and contracts can be evaluated as its own economic proposition.

Two Different Analytical Starting Points

Corporate debt and project finance do not necessarily analyse risk the same way. The distinction can be summarised as two different paths from the borrowing entity to debt repayment.

What Makes a Project Financeable

  • Repayment capacity has to sit somewhere specific. An established company expanding can often be evaluated against its overall financial profile; a dedicated project deploying capital before operations begin usually needs its own cash flows to carry the analysis.
  • The project needs an identifiable economic framework — total cost, development timeline, what generates income, whether revenue and key costs are contractually supported, and when cash generation actually begins.
  • Construction risk changes the conversation. Delays, cost overruns and approval gaps matter as much as the eventual returns — lenders look hard at who's executing, what the schedule is, and how overruns get funded.
  • Revenue visibility is sector-specific but always central — power purchase agreements and tariffs for renewable energy, concession terms and traffic assumptions for infrastructure, occupancy and rates for hospitality, capacity utilisation and demand for industrial projects.
  • Risk allocation has to be explicit. Land risk, approval risk, construction risk, demand risk — for each one, who bears it, what protects against it, and what happens if it materialises anyway.
  • An SPV creates a clean legal structure, but not financeability on its own — the underlying project still needs defined assets, credible contracts and analysable cash flows.
  • Sponsor strength still matters, especially during development when project cash flows don't exist yet — execution capability and equity contribution sit alongside the project economics, not apart from them.
  • The cash-flow waterfall is a structural question in itself — how revenue moves through operating costs, statutory obligations, debt service and reserves before any surplus is available.
  • The financial model has to go deeper than a standard corporate model — cost and funding schedule during development, revenue and operating assumptions once live, and sensitivity analysis testing whether the economics actually support the proposed debt.

EquiFin Perspective

The choice between corporate debt and project finance should not begin with the financing product. It should begin with understanding where the economic risk sits and where the repayment capacity will come from.

Key Takeaways

  • Project finance is not defined simply by the size of the capital expenditure.
  • The key distinction often lies in where the primary repayment capacity is expected to come from.
  • Corporate debt focuses primarily on the broader company’s financial strength and cash flows.
  • Project-oriented financing places greater emphasis on project economics, implementation and future project cash flows.
  • Construction, approval and cost-overrun risks can materially influence financing discussions.
  • Revenue visibility and contractual structures can be important to evaluating project repayment capacity.
  • Project financing requires detailed analysis of the complete lifecycle—from development through operations and debt servicing.
  • The right financing approach should follow the economic characteristics of the transaction rather than the size of the funding requirement alone.

Closing Perspective

A major capital investment can be transformational for a business — but before deciding how to finance it, management should understand what the capital is actually supporting. Is the business borrowing against the strength of its existing enterprise, or developing an asset with its own lifecycle, risks and future cash flows? That answer shapes far more than the financing source; it shapes the entire transaction. The effective starting point isn't which financing product to use — it's the economic structure of the investment, where repayment capacity actually sits, and what framework reflects that reality. That's where project financing begins.
