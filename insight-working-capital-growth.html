Why Growing Businesses Often Need More Funding Than Expected

Revenue growth is usually viewed as a positive business outcome. But growth can also consume cash. As sales increase, businesses may need to fund higher inventory, larger receivables and a more complex operating cycle—often before customer cash is received.

10 Min Read·Perspective·September 2026

The Question

A company is growing. Orders are increasing. Revenue is improving. New customers are being added. Production capacity is expanding. On paper, the business is performing well. Yet the CFO is under increasing pressure to arrange additional funding. The question naturally arises: if the business is growing and profitable, why is there still a shortage of cash?

The answer often lies in working capital. Growth requires more than the ability to generate sales — it may also require the business to finance the operating cycle behind those sales. A company may need to purchase more raw materials, hold higher inventory, extend more credit to customers, fund additional production and support a larger distribution network. But customer payments may arrive weeks or months later. This creates a funding gap, and the faster the business grows, the larger that gap can become.

Growth Does Not Automatically Improve Liquidity

One of the most important financial distinctions for a growing business is the difference between growth in revenue and growth in available cash — they do not always move together. Consider a simplified example: a company increases annual revenue from ₹500 crore to ₹750 crore. This appears positive, but supporting that additional ₹250 crore of revenue may require ₹40 crore of additional inventory, ₹50 crore of additional receivables and ₹20 crore of additional operating expenditure. The business may therefore need to deploy significant capital before receiving cash from the additional sales — the growth itself creates a financing requirement. The timing difference between higher procurement, higher inventory, higher sales, higher receivables and eventual customer collection is where working capital pressure emerges.

Working Capital Is the Capital Required to Keep the Business Moving

At a practical level, working capital supports the day-to-day movement of the business — connected to inventory, receivables, payables, operating expenses and cash conversion cycles. The precise working capital requirement differs significantly between sectors. Manufacturing may require funding for raw materials, work-in-progress, finished goods and customer receivables. Trading businesses may experience pressure from inventory cycles, supplier payment terms and customer credit periods. Infrastructure contractors may face long billing cycles, retention money and delayed receivables. Healthcare businesses may manage insurance receivables, institutional receivables and operating inventory. Consumer businesses may experience seasonal inventory build-up, distribution cycles and retailer receivables. There is no single working capital formula that applies to every business — the requirement needs to be understood within the context of the operating model.

The Cash Conversion Cycle Explains the Funding Requirement

The longer this cycle takes, the longer the company’s capital remains tied up. A business may pay suppliers in 30 days but customers may pay after 90 days — that creates a funding gap, and if the business grows, the amount tied up in this gap can increase.

02 — Receivable Growth Can Create a Hidden Liquidity Problem

Revenue growth is often celebrated. Receivable growth deserves equal attention. A company may report strong sales growth, but if receivables grow faster than revenue, cash conversion may weaken. Consider the difference between a scenario where revenue increases by 25% with receivables increasing proportionately and collection behaviour remaining stable, versus a scenario where revenue increases by 25% but receivables increase by 50%, collection periods extend, and customer concentration increases. The reported revenue growth may look similar; the liquidity impact is very different. Management should therefore regularly ask whether receivables are growing faster than sales, whether the average collection period has increased, whether large balances are concentrated with a few customers, whether receivables are genuinely collectible, and whether customer payment terms are changing. Revenue recorded in financial statements is not the same as cash received in the bank.

03 — Inventory Can Consume Capital Before It Creates Revenue

Inventory is necessary for many businesses, but it represents capital already deployed. A company may increase inventory because sales are expected to grow, supply chains are uncertain, lead times have increased, new products are being launched, or seasonal demand is approaching. Each decision may have a valid business rationale, but higher inventory also means more cash is tied up. The question is not simply whether inventory is increasing — it is why inventory is increasing, and how quickly it will convert into sales and cash. Slow-moving inventory can create a particular problem: the company has already spent cash, but the asset may not convert into revenue within the expected cycle, which can create pressure even when overall business performance appears healthy.

04 — Growth Can Require Capital Before Revenue Is Earned

Businesses often think of growth as a future source of cash, but the investment required for growth usually happens first. Consider a manufacturing company receiving a large new order — before recognising revenue, it may need to procure additional raw material, increase production, hold additional work-in-progress, manufacture finished goods, dispatch the products, wait for customer acceptance and wait for payment. Revenue may be recognised before the final cash collection. This creates an important timing issue: the business needs liquidity during the period between deployment and collection, which is why growth can increase funding requirements before it improves cash balances.

05 — EBITDA Growth Does Not Solve Working Capital Automatically

A growing company may report higher revenue, higher EBITDA and improved profitability, but working capital pressure can still increase. This connects directly with a broader financial principle: profitability and liquidity are not the same thing. EBITDA measures operating performance; working capital determines how much capital is required to support the operating cycle. A company can therefore experience rising revenue, EBITDA and profitability while simultaneously experiencing rising inventory, receivables, working capital and cash pressure — not necessarily a contradiction, but a normal feature of many growth cycles. The issue is whether management has anticipated the capital requirement.

06 — Rapid Growth Can Become a Financing Risk

Growth is generally desirable, but poorly funded growth can create financial stress. Imagine a business that wins significantly more orders and management increases procurement, production, staffing and distribution — but the company does not arrange sufficient incremental working capital. The result may be more orders, more operating activity, a higher working capital requirement, insufficient liquidity, supplier pressure and operational disruption. The business does not fail because demand disappeared — it faces pressure because growth consumed capital faster than financing was arranged. This is why capital planning should be integrated with commercial planning, asking what additional working capital will be required to support forecast growth.

07 — Working Capital Needs to Be Analysed Customer by Customer

Aggregate numbers can sometimes hide important differences. A business may have a healthy overall receivable position, but a significant portion may be concentrated with one or two customers, and customer relationships may have very different credit characteristics — a large-volume customer with a 30-day payment cycle and established collection history looks very different from a rapidly growing customer with a 90-day payment cycle and limited historical experience. The revenue opportunity may be attractive, but the working capital implications are different. Management should therefore consider not only total sales growth but also customer concentration, credit terms, collection history, dispute patterns and counterparty quality — particularly important when evaluating receivables-based financing or supply chain structures.

08 — Supplier Terms Can Be a Strategic Part of Working Capital

Working capital is not determined only by how quickly customers pay — supplier payment terms also matter. A business that pays suppliers immediately but collects from customers after 90 days has a significant funding requirement; another business may negotiate supplier terms that better align with customer collections. Reducing this gap can improve liquidity. However, payment terms should not be viewed purely as a financing tool — supplier relationships, commercial credibility and supply continuity also matter. The objective is not simply to delay every payment; it is to create an operating cycle that is economically sustainable.

09 — Seasonal Businesses Need Seasonal Liquidity Planning

Some businesses experience significant fluctuations in working capital requirements — consumer products, agriculture-linked businesses, hospitality, retail and certain manufacturing segments. A company may require significant inventory before the peak season, with cash deployed months before sales occur. Once the selling cycle begins, inventory converts into revenue and eventually collections, creating a working capital curve rather than a constant requirement — from low season, through inventory build-up, to peak working capital requirement, sales season, collection and cash release. A financing structure that ignores seasonality can create unnecessary pressure; the funding requirement should ideally reflect the actual operating cycle.

Why “More Working Capital” Is Not Always the Correct Solution

When liquidity pressure emerges, the immediate response is often that a larger working capital limit is needed. Sometimes that is correct, but the underlying cause should first be understood — the funding requirement may have increased because of genuine revenue growth, longer customer collection periods, excess inventory, operational inefficiency, margin pressure, delayed billing or project cost overruns. These situations require different responses. Increasing borrowing capacity may address the immediate liquidity gap, but it may not resolve the underlying cause — if receivables are increasing because of collection delays, additional borrowing may temporarily support the business, but improving collections may also be necessary. Working capital financing and working capital management need to work together.

10 — The Quality of Working Capital Matters to Lenders

When evaluating working capital financing, lenders generally look beyond the total amount requested. They may seek to understand receivables — ageing, customer concentration, collection trends and credit quality; inventory — movement, obsolescence risk, seasonality and valuation; payables — supplier terms, outstanding periods and dependency; and sales — historical trends, growth assumptions and customer profile. The lender is trying to understand whether the operating assets being funded are genuinely connected to the business cycle. The question is not simply how much funding the company needs — it is what is creating the requirement, and how the underlying assets will convert back into cash.

A Practical Example

Consider a company with ₹1,000 crore annual revenue. Management expects revenue to grow by 30%, suggesting additional revenue of ₹300 crore. But suppose the operating model requires 60 days inventory, 75 days receivables and 45 days supplier credit — the additional revenue may require substantial incremental capital within the operating cycle. The exact funding requirement depends on gross margins, procurement structure, collection terms and inventory intensity, but the broader lesson is clear: revenue forecasts should be accompanied by working capital forecasts. A growth plan without a liquidity plan is incomplete.

EquiFin Perspective

Growth consumes capital before it creates cash. Businesses that plan only for revenue growth can find themselves underprepared for the liquidity requirement that sits behind it.

Key Takeaways

  • Revenue growth and cash generation do not always move together.
  • Growing businesses often need additional capital to fund inventory, receivables and operating activity before customer cash is received.
  • Working capital pressure can increase even when revenue and EBITDA are growing.
  • Receivable quality and collection cycles are as important as headline sales growth.
  • Inventory growth should be analysed to distinguish genuine business requirements from operational inefficiency.
  • Supplier payment terms influence the size of the operating funding gap.
  • Seasonal businesses may require financing structures that accommodate temporary peaks in working capital.
  • A growth strategy should include a working capital strategy.

Closing Perspective

For many businesses, the biggest financial pressure does not emerge when growth slows. It emerges when growth accelerates faster than liquidity planning. More orders require more inventory. More production requires more procurement. More sales can create larger receivables. Cash is deployed before it returns. The business may be growing successfully while simultaneously becoming more dependent on external capital.

That is why the right question is not simply how fast the business can grow. It should also be how much capital that growth will consume before it begins generating cash. Understanding that relationship can help promoters and CFOs plan growth with greater financial discipline — because growth is not funded by revenue. For a significant part of the operating cycle, it is funded by capital.
