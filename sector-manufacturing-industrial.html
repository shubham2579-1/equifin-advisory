Manufacturing & Industrial

Manufacturing Growth Consumes Capital Before It Creates Scale.

Manufacturing businesses often require capital at multiple points simultaneously. A company may be expanding capacity while funding higher inventory, extending credit to customers and servicing existing debt.

The financing challenge is therefore not simply to arrange a term loan or increase a working capital limit. It is to ensure that capacity expansion, operating cash flow and balance-sheet leverage can grow together. EquiFin helps manufacturing and industrial businesses evaluate capital requirements through the lens of operational performance, cash conversion and lender underwriting.

A Manufacturing Business Can Grow Revenue and Still Experience a Capital Shortage

Growth in manufacturing is capital-intensive. Higher production may require more raw material, more inventory, additional machinery, larger receivable exposure and higher working capital limits. This creates a timing gap.

New Order → Raw Material Purchase → Production → Finished Goods Inventory → Customer Dispatch → Receivable Collection → Cash Realisation

The business spends cash long before it collects revenue. At the same time, expansion may require additional fixed capital. Manufacturing finance is therefore a balance between funding the plant and funding the cycle that keeps the plant running.

The Manufacturing Capital Landscape

Manufacturing Businesses Need Different Capital for Different Balance-Sheet Requirements

Manufacturing capital is usually not solved through one facility. Different requirements may require different financing structures — commercial banks, consortium or multiple-banking arrangements, NBFCs and ECBs are all relevant parts of the manufacturing capital landscape.

Long-Term Capital

Used for plant expansion, machinery, technology upgrades and new manufacturing facilities.

Working Capital Facilities

Used to support raw material, work-in-progress, finished goods and receivables.

Equipment Finance

Relevant for machinery and specialised production assets.

Non-Fund Facilities

Depending on the business model — Letters of Credit and Bank Guarantees.

Refinancing

Potentially relevant when existing debt is expensive or poorly aligned with current cash flows.

EquiFin Perspective

Manufacturing businesses do not face one capital cycle. They operate with two simultaneously — the long-term cycle of capacity investment and the short-term cycle of working capital conversion.

Where Manufacturing Businesses Typically Need Capital

Capital requirements usually appear at specific operating and growth events.

01

Greenfield Manufacturing Expansion

A new manufacturing facility may require capital for land and building, plant and machinery, utilities, installation and pre-operative expenditure. The challenge is that debt servicing may begin before the plant reaches optimal utilisation. The financing structure must account for the difference between project completion and commercial stabilisation.

02

Brownfield Capacity Expansion

An existing manufacturer may not need a completely new plant. Instead, growth may require additional production lines, debottlenecking, automation or machinery replacement. Brownfield expansion can be financially attractive because existing infrastructure may already support the new capacity. But lenders still ask: will incremental capacity produce incremental cash flow quickly enough to justify additional leverage?

03

Machinery and Technology Upgrades

Manufacturing competitiveness can depend on production technology. Capital expenditure may be required to improve capacity, efficiency, quality and automation. The financing case must demonstrate whether the investment will create higher volumes, better margins or lower operating costs.

04

Working Capital Growth

Revenue growth generally increases working capital requirements. Higher turnover may require more inventory, higher receivables and larger procurement commitments. This is one of the most important financing realities for growing manufacturers: revenue growth can consume liquidity before it improves cash flow.

05

Order Book Execution

A strong order book may create positive business visibility. But execution requires capital. Before the company recognises revenue, it may need to fund procurement, production, labour and logistics. The financing question becomes: does the business have sufficient banking capacity to execute the growth it has already won?

06

Refinancing Existing Debt

A manufacturer may carry legacy debt raised for earlier expansion, machinery acquisition or working capital. Over time, the original structure may become inefficient — high borrowing cost, short repayment profile, excessive dependence on NBFC debt or fragmented banking facilities.

The Core Manufacturing Financing Challenge

Capacity Does Not Automatically Create Cash Flow

A manufacturing company can invest heavily in capacity. But the financial outcome depends on utilisation.

Installed Capacity → Capacity Utilisation → Production Volume → Sales → EBITDA → Operating Cash Flow → Debt Servicing Capacity

This is why a lender does not finance machinery simply because it has collateral value. The lender needs to understand: what level of production will the asset achieve, and what cash flow will that production generate?

Signature Insight

How Lenders Underwrite Manufacturing Businesses

What does a manufacturing credit committee actually evaluate? Manufacturing underwriting usually combines three perspectives — business performance (can the company continue selling its products?), operating efficiency (can the company manufacture profitably?) and financial resilience (can cash flows support existing and proposed debt?). Capacity utilisation, order-book visibility, customer concentration, machinery quality and environmental approvals are important underwriting considerations.

1. Capacity Utilisation

Installed capacity alone does not demonstrate financial strength. A lender wants to understand current utilisation, historical utilisation and expansion assumptions. Capacity utilisation of approximately 70% or above is an indicative underwriting consideration in manufacturing transactions, while actual expectations vary by sector and transaction.

The key question: is the company expanding because existing capacity is genuinely constrained — or because future demand is only assumed?

2. Order Book and Revenue Visibility

For contract-driven manufacturers, order-book visibility can provide insight into future production requirements. However, lenders also evaluate order quality, customer concentration, cancellation risk and execution capacity. An order book is useful only when it can be converted into revenue → EBITDA → cash flow.

3. Customer Concentration

A manufacturer may have strong revenue but limited customer diversification. If one or two customers represent a significant portion of sales, negotiating power may be weak, receivables may become concentrated and demand disruption can affect the entire business. Lender question: how dependent is the company's cash flow on a small number of counterparties?

4. Raw Material Exposure

Manufacturing margins can be affected by commodity prices, import dependence and supplier concentration. If raw material prices increase but the company cannot immediately pass the increase to customers, margins can compress:

Raw Material Price Increase → Margin Compression → Lower EBITDA → Reduced Cash Flow → Debt Servicing Pressure

5. Plant and Machinery Quality

Lenders may assess machinery age, technology, replacement requirements and operational efficiency. The question is not only what the machinery is worth — it is also whether the machinery can continue supporting competitive production over the financing period.

6. Working Capital Discipline

Manufacturing businesses can become financially stressed even when EBITDA remains positive, because profits may remain locked in inventory and receivables. The lender therefore evaluates inventory days, receivable days, creditor days and the working capital cycle.

Signature Insight

The Manufacturing Cash Conversion Cycle

EBITDA Does Not Automatically Mean Liquidity

Consider two manufacturers with identical EBITDA. One collects receivables in 45 days. The other collects in 120 days. Their liquidity profiles are fundamentally different.

Cash → Raw Material → Work in Progress → Finished Goods → Sales → Receivables → Cash

The longer this cycle takes, the greater the working capital requirement.

EquiFin Perspective

For manufacturing businesses, the speed at which EBITDA converts into cash can be as important as the EBITDA itself.

Inventory: Asset or Locked Capital?

Inventory Supports Production — But Excess Inventory Consumes Liquidity

Manufacturers need inventory for operational continuity. But excess inventory can create pressure — slow-moving stock, demand slowdown, forecasting errors or obsolescence. Inventory obsolescence and working capital leakage are key credit committee concerns.

Lenders therefore evaluate not only the quantity of inventory but also quality, ageing and convertibility.

Key Question

How much of the reported inventory can realistically support working capital borrowing?

Receivables: Revenue Is Not Cash Until It Is Collected

Growing Sales Can Create a Larger Funding Requirement

A manufacturing business may extend credit to customers to support sales. But longer collection cycles can increase borrowing requirements.

Higher Sales → Higher Receivables → More Capital Locked → Higher Working Capital Utilisation

The challenge is particularly relevant when a company grows faster than its banking limits.

EquiFin Perspective

A business can outgrow its working capital structure even while remaining profitable.

Capacity Utilisation: The Bridge Between Capex and Debt Service

New Capacity Must Eventually Produce Enough Cash to Service the Debt Used to Build It

A manufacturing expansion creates additional fixed assets, debt obligations, depreciation and operating costs. The financial model must evaluate:

New Capacity → Utilisation Ramp-Up → Incremental Production → Incremental Revenue → Incremental EBITDA → Cash Available for Debt Service

The most important assumption is often not the final capacity. It is: how quickly will the business reach meaningful utilisation?

Greenfield vs Brownfield Expansion

The Capital Requirement May Look Similar. The Risk Profile Does Not.

Greenfield

Typically involves a new facility, new infrastructure, longer execution period and greater ramp-up uncertainty.

Brownfield

Typically involves existing operations, existing management, existing market access and incremental capacity.

From a lender's perspective: an existing operating history can reduce uncertainty — but expansion debt must still be justified by future demand and cash-flow capacity.

Machinery Finance

Machinery Creates Productive Capacity, but Debt Must Be Supported by Business Cash Flow

Equipment financing may be relevant for production lines, specialised machinery, automation equipment and process upgrades. Machinery term loans, Capex LCs and supplier credit are among relevant manufacturing financing structures. However, machinery financing should be evaluated alongside existing leverage, working capital utilisation and future maintenance capex.

Important Principle

The ability to mortgage a machine does not automatically determine the company's ability to repay the loan.

Signature Insight

Working Capital and Capex Must Be Planned Together

A Term Loan Can Solve the Expansion — but Create Pressure on the Operating Cycle

This is a common manufacturing problem. A company raises a term loan for new machinery and capacity expansion. Production increases. But higher production requires more raw material, more inventory and more receivable funding. The company may therefore face:

Capex Expansion + Working Capital Growth = Total Capital Requirement

A financing plan that only funds the fixed asset may be incomplete.

EquiFin Perspective

The true cost of manufacturing expansion includes both the machinery that creates capacity and the working capital required to utilise it.

Important Manufacturing Underwriting Metrics

Lenders use financial metrics to understand operating reality.

DSCR

Measures whether cash generation can support scheduled debt obligations. DSCR of approximately 1.30x or higher is an indicative manufacturing credit benchmark.

Debt / EBITDA

Provides a view of leverage relative to operating earnings. The metric must be interpreted alongside business cyclicality, working capital intensity and capex requirements.

TOL / ATNW

Total Outside Liabilities relative to Adjusted Tangible Net Worth. Indicative expectations of approximately 2.5x–3.0x or below apply in manufacturing underwriting, depending on the transaction.

Current Ratio

Measures short-term balance-sheet liquidity. Approximately 1.33x is an indicative benchmark.

Capacity Utilisation

Shows whether installed capacity is being effectively used.

Inventory Days

Measures capital locked in stock.

Receivable Days

Measures how quickly revenue converts into cash.

Customer Concentration

Measures dependency on individual customers.

No single metric determines manufacturing bankability. Lenders evaluate whether the metrics together describe a financially sustainable operating business.

Security and Collateral Structure

Manufacturing Finance Is Often Supported by Both Fixed and Current Assets

Typical security structures include pari-passu charge on fixed assets, hypothecation of raw materials and hypothecation of book debts, alongside inventory margins as part of working capital structures.

Term Debt Security

Plant & machinery, land & building, fixed assets.

Working Capital Security

Inventory, receivables, current assets.

But collateral alone does not create credit approval. Security provides downside protection. Cash flow remains central to debt repayment.

Consortium and Multiple Banking

As Businesses Grow, Banking Relationships Can Become More Complex

A manufacturing company may eventually require larger banking limits than one institution is comfortable providing. This can lead to consortium banking (multiple lenders sharing facilities under a coordinated structure) or multiple banking (different lenders providing different banking facilities) — multi-bank consortium working capital facilities and multi-bank limit integration are common manufacturing transaction scenarios.

The CFO challenge is not simply adding more banks. It is managing security sharing, documentation, inter-creditor coordination and information consistency.

Raw Material Volatility

Margin Risk Can Become Credit Risk

Many manufacturers operate with exposure to volatile inputs. If raw material prices increase sharply, gross margins can compress and additional working capital may be required. Raw material price volatility is a key manufacturing sector risk. The lender may therefore assess pricing power, customer contracts, pass-through mechanisms and historical margin volatility.

Demand Cyclicality

Historical Financial Performance Does Not Guarantee Future Capacity Utilisation

Certain manufacturing sectors can experience demand cycles, industry downturns and customer order volatility. The financing structure should therefore be evaluated under more than one scenario.

Base Case

Demand performs according to plan.

Moderate Stress

Capacity utilisation falls.

Severe Stress

Margins and collections deteriorate simultaneously.

The question is: how much stress can the company's capital structure absorb before liquidity becomes constrained?

Refinancing and Debt Rationalisation

Legacy Debt Structures Can Limit Future Growth

Manufacturing businesses often accumulate debt over time — for example, a machinery loan plus an NBFC term loan plus a working capital bank facility plus an equipment facility, creating a fragmented capital structure. As the company grows, management may need to evaluate cost of debt, repayment schedules, security duplication and banking relationships.

The objective is not always to increase borrowing. Sometimes it is to improve the efficiency of the debt already on the balance sheet.

When Manufacturing Growth Becomes a Capital Structure Issue

Revenue Growth Can Hide Balance-Sheet Stress

Consider a manufacturer experiencing 30% revenue growth, higher capacity utilisation and a larger order book. This may appear financially positive. But simultaneously, receivables may increase, inventory may rise, working capital borrowing may reach limits and term debt may increase.

The key question becomes: is the balance sheet growing at a sustainable pace relative to the operating business?

Frequently Asked Questions

How are manufacturing businesses typically financed in India?

Manufacturing businesses may use a combination of term loans for plant and machinery, working capital facilities for inventory and receivables, equipment finance and other transaction-specific structures. The appropriate mix depends on the company's operating cycle, capex requirement and debt-servicing capacity.

What do lenders evaluate before financing a manufacturing expansion?

Lenders typically evaluate historical financial performance, capacity utilisation, order-book visibility, customer concentration, projected cash flows, existing leverage, DSCR and the working capital cycle.

Why does manufacturing growth increase working capital requirements?

Higher production generally requires more raw materials, inventory and customer credit. Cash is therefore deployed before the company collects revenue from increased sales.

What is the difference between greenfield and brownfield manufacturing expansion?

Greenfield expansion involves establishing new manufacturing infrastructure, while brownfield expansion adds or upgrades capacity within an existing operating environment. The two structures can have different execution, ramp-up and financing risks.

Why is capacity utilisation important for lenders?

Capacity utilisation helps lenders assess whether existing assets are being effectively used and whether future expansion assumptions are supported by actual demand and operating performance.

Can machinery be financed separately from the overall business?

Machinery can be financed through equipment or term loan structures, but lenders typically still evaluate the broader company's financial strength and ability to generate sufficient cash flow for repayment.

When should a manufacturing company consider refinancing?

Refinancing may be relevant when existing debt has become expensive, fragmented or poorly aligned with the company's current operating cash flows and maturity profile.
