Inside the Lender’s Credit Decision

A lender does not evaluate a borrowing proposal only by looking at collateral or profitability. Credit decisions are built around a broader question: how confidently can the institution understand the business, the transaction and the source of repayment?

11 Min Read·Perspective·September 2026

The Question

A company approaches a lender for capital — the requirement may be for business expansion, capacity creation, acquisition, project development, working capital, refinancing or real estate funding. Management prepares financial statements, a presentation is shared, the funding requirement is explained, and security is discussed. Yet the transaction may still take longer than expected. The lender asks additional questions. More information is requested. Assumptions are challenged. Financial projections are reviewed.

At times, management may feel that the company is profitable, so why is the lender analysing so many things? Because a lending decision is not simply about determining whether a business is successful. A lender is trying to answer a more specific question: can we understand the risk clearly enough to become comfortable with the proposed capital structure and the source of repayment? That process involves multiple layers of analysis.

A Lender Is Evaluating a Credit Story

Promoters often approach financing from the perspective of capital requirement — we need ₹X crore for expansion. The lender approaches the same transaction differently: the institution needs to understand a complete credit story, one that connects the business, its business performance, the capital requirement, the transaction structure, cash-flow generation, debt servicing and risk mitigation. If one part of this story is unclear, the lender may become cautious. This does not necessarily mean the business is weak — it may mean the transaction has not yet been presented in a sufficiently coherent way.

The First Question: Why Does the Business Need Capital?

The funding requirement itself is an important part of the credit assessment. A lender will generally want clarity on what the capital is being used for, why it is required now, how the amount was determined, what happens if the capital is not raised, and what economic outcome is expected. For example, “business expansion” is not always sufficient explanation — a stronger transaction narrative explains that the company is expanding production capacity from X to Y to support identified demand, that the total capital requirement includes plant and machinery, implementation costs and incremental working capital, and that the expansion is expected to increase operating capacity and generate additional cash flows over a defined period. One is a funding request. The other is a financing rationale.

01 — Business Quality Comes Before Financial Ratios

Financial ratios are important, but numbers do not exist independently from the business. Before assessing leverage or debt servicing, a lender may first try to understand what the company actually does, how it generates revenue, who its customers are, what drives margins, what differentiates the business, and what the key risks are. A lender needs to understand the underlying economic engine — two companies may report similar revenue and EBITDA, but one may have long-term customer relationships, diversified revenue, stable margins and established market positioning, while the other has customer concentration, volatile margins, limited operating history and high dependence on a single contract. The financial numbers may initially appear similar; the credit assessment may not be. A lender finances future repayment capacity, so understanding the business is essential.

02 — Historical Performance Establishes the Starting Point

Historical financial statements help lenders understand how the business has performed through time — revenue trends, EBITDA margins, profitability, net worth, cash generation, working capital movement, existing debt and contingent obligations. But historical numbers are not reviewed only as a compliance exercise. The lender may look for patterns — is growth consistent and explainable, and what explains movements in EBITDA margin over time? The objective is to understand the story behind the numbers. Unexpected movements are not automatically negative, but they generally require explanation.

03 — Cash Flow Matters More Than Accounting Profit Alone

One of the most important distinctions in credit analysis is the difference between profit and cash available for debt servicing. A company can report strong profitability while experiencing pressure on liquidity, because accounting profit may not immediately translate into cash — cash may be tied up in receivables, inventory, capex, project development or other operating requirements. Following the chain from revenue through operating profit, working capital movement, capex and other cash requirements, the lender wants to understand how much cash remains available for debt servicing. This is why cash-flow analysis is central to lending decisions.

04 — The Source of Repayment Must Be Clear

Every borrowing proposal should ultimately answer one fundamental question: how will the debt be repaid? This may sound obvious, but transaction presentations sometimes focus heavily on loan amount, asset value, security and interest rate while giving less attention to repayment. From a credit perspective, repayment capacity is central — the source may be corporate operating cash flows for an established operating company, project cash flows for a specific infrastructure or asset-backed project, contractual receivables for certain transaction structures, or asset cash flows for lease rental or income-generating assets. The lender will want to understand whether the proposed source of repayment is identifiable, sustainable, adequate and reasonably predictable. Security can provide additional comfort, but the underlying source of repayment remains important.

05 — Financial Projections Are Tested, Not Simply Accepted

Management may prepare projections showing revenue growth, margin improvement, cash-flow generation and debt reduction — important, but lenders generally do not treat management forecasts as guaranteed outcomes. The assumptions behind them are analysed: is revenue growth supported by existing demand, confirmed orders, capacity expansion or new markets; what will cause margins to improve — scale, pricing, product mix or cost reduction; and will growth require additional inventory, receivables or operating capital? The lender is trying to distinguish between a financial forecast and a financially credible forecast.

06 — Leverage Is About Capacity, Not Just a Ratio

Debt-to-equity and other leverage ratios are commonly used in credit analysis, but a ratio alone does not provide the full answer. Consider two companies with similar leverage — one with stable recurring cash flows, diversified customers and a long operating history; the other with volatile revenue, significant customer concentration and high working capital intensity. The same leverage ratio may represent different levels of risk. Leverage should therefore be considered alongside cash-flow stability, business risk, sector characteristics, growth stage and asset profile. The key question is how much debt this specific business can realistically support — not what the highest amount that can theoretically be borrowed is.

07 — Debt Servicing Capacity Is Tested Across Time

A company may have sufficient cash flow in aggregate, but debt obligations occur on specific dates — this creates the importance of timing. A lender may examine operating cash flow against interest obligations, principal repayment, other financial commitments and available headroom, to determine whether cash flows remain adequate throughout the financing period. A business may appear capable of repaying debt over seven years in total, but if repayment obligations are concentrated in the first three years, pressure may emerge. This is why repayment schedules and projected cash flows need to be analysed together.

08 — Working Capital Behaviour Can Influence Credit Comfort

For many businesses, working capital is a major part of credit analysis. Lenders may review receivables — ageing, collection patterns and customer concentration; inventory — turnover, movement and obsolescence risk; payables — payment behaviour and supplier dependency; and the cash conversion cycle — how long capital remains deployed. A company may report revenue growth, but if receivables are increasing disproportionately, the lender may want to understand why. Similarly, strong EBITDA may not provide full comfort if operating cash is consistently absorbed by working capital. The lender is assessing not only profitability — it is assessing liquidity behaviour.

09 — Management Quality Is Part of the Credit Assessment

Numbers can explain what has happened. Management discussions often help lenders understand why it happened and what comes next. Credit evaluation may therefore consider promoter experience, management capability, execution track record, financial discipline, governance practices and strategic clarity. This does not mean a lender is looking for a perfect business history — businesses face challenges, markets change, projects get delayed, margins fluctuate. What often matters is management’s ability to understand problems, explain them clearly, demonstrate corrective action and maintain financial discipline. A credible management narrative can materially improve the quality of a transaction discussion.

10 — Sector Risk Influences the Credit Lens

Lenders do not evaluate every industry using exactly the same framework. In manufacturing, potential focus areas include capacity utilisation, raw material dependency, customer diversification and margin stability. In real estate, land title, approvals, project execution and sales velocity. In infrastructure, concession structure, implementation, counterparty and long-term cash flows. In healthcare, occupancy, payer mix, operating margins and expansion requirements. The numbers may be similar; the underlying credit questions can be different, which is why sector understanding is important in transaction preparation — a generic financing presentation may not adequately address sector-specific concerns.

11 — Security Is Important, But It Does Not Replace Credit Quality

Promoters sometimes approach financing with a collateral-first mindset — we have valuable assets, why is the lender concerned about cash flows? Because asset value and repayment capacity serve different purposes. Security can provide risk mitigation, recovery support and additional transaction comfort, but lending institutions also need to assess whether the business can service the obligation. The primary question remains how the debt will be repaid; security is the secondary protection if things do not go as planned. The strongest transactions generally demonstrate both a credible source of repayment and appropriate risk mitigation.

12 — The Transaction Structure Can Change the Credit Outcome

The same business can present different credit profiles depending on how a transaction is structured. Consider a ₹100 crore requirement — short-term borrowing for a long-term asset versus longer-term financing aligned with asset cash flows. The business is identical, the requirement is identical, but the financing risk may not be. Transaction structuring may also influence debt servicing burden, security utilisation, cash-flow pressure, covenant headroom and future borrowing capacity. This is why lender engagement should not begin and end with the maximum amount available — a more useful discussion asks what transaction structure the economics of the business can support.

13 — Lenders Need to Understand What Can Go Wrong

Credit analysis is not only about the base case. Lenders also consider downside scenarios — revenue growing slower than expected, margins declining, customer payments delayed, project implementation taking longer, input costs increasing, or interest costs rising. The question becomes what happens to debt servicing capacity under a lower-performance case. Sensitivity analysis helps identify whether the financing structure has adequate resilience. The objective is not to prove that no downside can occur — it is to understand the consequences if reasonable downside scenarios materialise.

Why Good Businesses Can Still Receive Difficult Credit Responses

A business can be fundamentally strong and still face challenges in a financing process. Possible reasons include an unclear funding requirement, inadequately explained projected cash flows, a repayment structure that does not align with the business cycle, inconsistent financial information, difficult-to-validate key assumptions, unaddressed sector-specific risks, or a mismatch between the lender and the transaction. This is important: a difficult credit process does not always mean the business is not financeable. Sometimes it means the transaction needs better preparation, positioning or structuring.

The Importance of Lender Fit

Not every lender evaluates every opportunity through the same lens. Different institutions may have different preferences regarding sector, ticket size, risk appetite, tenor, security and project stage. Therefore, financing is not simply a process of approaching the largest possible number of lenders — a more strategic approach asks which institutions are likely to understand and evaluate this transaction most effectively. This can improve the quality of the financing process. The objective should not be maximum outreach. It should be relevant outreach.

The Credit Evaluation Framework

Actual credit processes are more detailed, but the central principle remains consistent — the lender needs to understand the relationship between business, cash flow, debt, repayment and risk.

StageWhat the Lender Is Testing
Business qualityIs the underlying economic model durable and understandable?
Historical performanceIs the track record consistent and explainable?
Capital requirementIs the use of funds clearly defined and rational?
Cash-flow analysisHow much cash is genuinely available after business needs?
Debt servicing capacityCan obligations be met across time, not just in aggregate?
Risk assessmentWhat could go wrong, and how resilient is the structure?
Transaction structuringDoes the structure reflect the economics of the requirement?

EquiFin Perspective

Lenders do not finance financial statements in isolation. They finance a view of future repayment capacity, based on their understanding of the business, the transaction and the risks that could affect both.

Key Takeaways

  • Lenders evaluate a complete credit story—not only the funding requirement.
  • Business quality and the underlying economic model are important before financial ratios are analysed.
  • Accounting profitability does not automatically translate into debt servicing capacity.
  • Every financing proposal should clearly explain the primary source of repayment.
  • Leverage must be considered alongside cash-flow stability and business risk.
  • Security is an important risk mitigant but does not replace the need for a credible repayment source.
  • Transaction structure can materially change how a lender views the financing risk.
  • Relevant lender fit can be as important as the quality of the business itself.

Closing Perspective

A borrowing proposal is often viewed internally as a capital requirement. For a lender, it is a risk assessment. The two perspectives meet when the transaction is presented clearly — a strong business may have attractive opportunities, but lenders need to understand why capital is required, how it will be deployed, what economic outcome it will create, where repayment will come from, and what could disrupt the plan.

The goal should therefore not simply be to convince a lender that the business is successful. It should be to help the lender understand why this transaction makes financial sense, how the capital will generate value, and how the resulting debt can realistically be serviced. That is the foundation of an effective credit story.
