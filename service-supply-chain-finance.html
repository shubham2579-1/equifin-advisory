Supply Chain Finance Is About More Than Funding One Company

Traditional working capital finance evaluates a company's own inventory, receivables, cash flows and collateral. Supply Chain Finance looks beyond a single balance sheet.

It examines the commercial ecosystem connecting: Buyer → Supplier → Manufacturer → Distributor → Dealer → Customer.

Each participant has a different cash-flow requirement. A supplier wants faster payment. A buyer may want to preserve payment terms. A distributor may require liquidity to purchase inventory. A lender may be willing to finance the transaction based partly on the strength and visibility of the underlying commercial relationship.

This creates a fundamentally different financing proposition. Instead of asking only "How creditworthy is the borrower?", Supply Chain Finance can also ask "How reliable is the underlying trade relationship and payment obligation?"

EquiFin positions Supply Chain Finance around institutional anchor programs, receivables financing and automated liquidity platforms — rather than generic short-term business borrowing.

Where Does Cash Get Trapped Across a Supply Chain?

A typical commercial transaction may look like this:

Step 1

Supplier Produces or Procures

The supplier commits capital towards raw materials, labour, production and logistics. Cash leaves the supplier.

Step 2

Goods or Services Are Delivered

The supplier completes the commercial obligation. An invoice is raised. But payment may still be 30, 60 or 90 days away.

Step 3

Buyer Receives Credit

The buyer benefits from agreed commercial payment terms. The supplier, however, continues to carry the receivable.

Step 4

Buyer Pays on Due Date

The commercial cycle is completed. But during the waiting period, the supplier's capital remains locked.

The Supply Chain Finance Opportunity — if the underlying transaction and buyer relationship are sufficiently credible, financing may help accelerate liquidity for the supplier without requiring the buyer to immediately shorten agreed payment terms. This can create a three-way benefit:

ParticipantPrimary Objective
Buyer / AnchorMaintain supply-chain stability
SupplierReceive earlier liquidity
Capital ProviderFinance validated commercial transactions

Supply Chain Finance therefore sits at the intersection of treasury management, commercial relationships and credit underwriting.

A Supplier's Credit Risk Is Not Always the Same as the Transaction's Credit Risk

A smaller supplier may have limited net worth, modest collateral, short operating history and restricted standalone borrowing capacity. But it may supply regularly to a large and financially established corporate buyer.

This creates an important financing distinction. The supplier's standalone balance sheet may not fully represent the quality of its underlying receivable. A capital provider may therefore also consider:

  • Who is the buyer?
  • Has the invoice been accepted?
  • Is the transaction genuine?
  • What is the historical payment behaviour?
  • Is there a history of disputes or deductions?

Anchor enterprise credit quality, vendor concentration, historical disputes and dilution rates, and digital invoice validation are key underwriting parameters.

Where Businesses Typically Need Supply Chain Finance

01

Suppliers Are Waiting Too Long for Payment

A supplier may have completed delivery but still wait 60–120 days for collections. During this period, the supplier may need to finance new production, raw materials, payroll and logistics. The challenge is particularly acute when supplier payment obligations occur before customer collections.

Financing requirement: accelerate access to liquidity against eligible receivables.

02

Buyers Want to Support Vendors Without Immediately Paying Earlier

A large corporate may depend on hundreds of suppliers. If smaller vendors face liquidity pressure, the consequences can affect production continuity, procurement reliability, vendor concentration and supply-chain resilience. The buyer may therefore seek a financing ecosystem that allows vendors to access earlier liquidity while preserving agreed payment terms — the strategic foundation of buyer-led supplier finance and reverse factoring structures.

03

Dealers Need Liquidity to Purchase Inventory

Manufacturers and large distributors often operate through dealer networks. A dealer may require capital to purchase inventory, maintain stock, support seasonal demand or expand distribution. Dealer financing can support the commercial movement of goods through the distribution network.

04

Receivables Are Growing Faster Than Internal Cash Generation

A company may experience strong sales growth. But if sales are increasingly on credit, the receivables book expands. The business can therefore become revenue-rich but liquidity-constrained. Receivables financing may help accelerate the conversion of eligible invoices into usable liquidity.

05

MSME Suppliers Have Limited Access to Traditional Bank Credit

Smaller suppliers may struggle to obtain sufficient standalone working capital limits. However, established commercial relationships with credible buyers can potentially create alternative financing pathways. This is where platforms and structures such as TReDS become particularly relevant.

Supply Chain Finance Structures

Different commercial relationships require different financing structures.

01 — Supplier Finance

Supplier Finance helps vendors access liquidity against eligible commercial receivables. The financing proposition may consider buyer quality, invoice validation, payment history and transaction documentation.

Objective: reduce the time between invoice generation and actual cash availability.

02 — Reverse Factoring / Payables Finance

Reverse Factoring is generally structured around a creditworthy buyer or anchor. The buyer's supplier ecosystem becomes the financing universe. Suppliers may have the option to receive earlier payment from a financing institution, while the buyer settles the obligation according to agreed terms.

Core advantage: the structure can leverage the commercial strength and payment obligation of the anchor rather than relying exclusively on the supplier's standalone credit profile.

03 — Receivables Financing

Receivables financing focuses on unlocking liquidity tied up in invoices. The lender may evaluate debtor quality, invoice validity, ageing, payment history and concentration. This structure can be relevant for companies with large institutional receivable books.

04 — Invoice Discounting

Invoice Discounting allows eligible invoices to be converted into earlier liquidity. However, the financing structure can vary significantly depending on recourse or non-recourse structure, buyer quality, invoice acceptance, tenor and dilution history. Invoice discounting should therefore not be presented as a commodity product — the underlying transaction quality matters.

05 — Dealer Finance

Dealer financing supports the downstream distribution ecosystem. Potential use cases include inventory purchases, seasonal stocking, dealer expansion and distribution growth. The financing structure may consider the relationship between manufacturer, distributor, dealer and underlying goods flow.

06 — Early Payment Programs

Early payment structures allow suppliers to receive payment before contractual maturity. The commercial objective may be to improve supplier liquidity, strengthen vendor relationships and improve supply-chain resilience.

07 — TReDS

The Trade Receivables Discounting System (TReDS) provides an institutional framework for financing eligible trade receivables through participating financiers and platform infrastructure. For eligible businesses, TReDS can create an additional pathway for receivable monetisation — particularly relevant for mid-market corporate anchors and MSME ecosystems.

Recourse vs Non-Recourse Financing

Who Ultimately Carries the Payment Risk?

One of the most important distinctions in receivables financing is whether the financing provider has recourse to the supplier if the underlying buyer does not pay.

Recourse Financing

Under a recourse structure, the supplier may retain repayment responsibility under defined circumstances. The financing provider's reliance is not exclusively on the buyer.

Non-Recourse Financing

Under a non-recourse structure, the financing risk allocation may be more closely linked to the buyer or underlying receivable obligation, subject to the specific transaction terms and exclusions.

Why the distinction matters — the structure affects pricing, credit assessment, risk allocation, accounting treatment and supplier obligations.

The right question is not which structure is cheaper? It is:

Which structure appropriately allocates credit and performance risk across the transaction?

The Anchor-Led Financing Model

A Strong Supply Chain Can Become a Financing Infrastructure

The most scalable Supply Chain Finance programs often begin with an anchor — a large corporate, manufacturer, procurement-intensive company, large distributor or institutional buyer. The anchor has an ecosystem of suppliers, vendors, dealers and distributors. Instead of each participant independently negotiating financing, an ecosystem-based structure can be explored:

Anchor → Approved Vendors / Suppliers → Validated Invoices / Purchase Transactions → Financing Institution / Platform → Accelerated Liquidity

The strength of this model comes from visibility. A financing institution can potentially evaluate anchor credit quality, transaction history, payment performance, supplier behaviour and invoice validation — rather than relying solely on fragmented borrower-level information.

What Do Capital Providers Actually Evaluate?

Supply Chain Finance lenders are not evaluating only a company's annual financial statements. They are often evaluating the quality of repeated commercial transactions.

1. Anchor Credit Quality

For anchor-led programs, the financial strength and payment capacity of the buyer can be central. Capital providers may assess financial profile, credit history, market position and payment behaviour.

2. Invoice Validation

The lender needs confidence that the underlying transaction is genuine. Validation may involve purchase order matching, delivery confirmation, invoice acceptance and digital verification. The objective is to reduce the risk of financing invoices that do not represent genuine commercial activity.

3. Historical Payment Behaviour

The quality of a buyer is not reflected only in financial statements. Actual payment behaviour matters. Capital providers may analyse average payment days, delays, historical defaults and dispute patterns.

4. Disputes & Dilution

An invoice may be raised for a certain amount. But the final payment may be reduced due to returns, quality disputes, credit notes or commercial adjustments. This difference can create dilution risk. Historical dispute and dilution patterns can therefore materially influence financing appetite.

5. Vendor Concentration

A program dependent on a small number of suppliers may carry concentration risk. Capital providers may examine supplier concentration, transaction volumes and dependency.

6. Transaction Tenor

Short-term commercial receivables are generally easier to analyse than long-duration or uncertain payment obligations. The financing tenor needs to align with the underlying trade cycle.

7. Digital Transaction Visibility

As Supply Chain Finance becomes more institutionalised, transaction data quality becomes increasingly important. Capital providers may prefer stronger visibility around invoices, purchase orders, delivery, acceptance and payment — digital invoice validation mechanisms are an important underwriting consideration.

The Supply Chain Finance Triangle

A financing relationship depends on three forms of confidence, shared between the supplier, the buyer / anchor and the capital provider:

Commercial Confidence

Is the underlying transaction genuine?

Credit Confidence

Can the relevant payment obligation be relied upon?

Data Confidence

Can the transaction be validated and monitored?

Supply Chain Finance works best when all three are visible — the supplier delivers goods or services to the buyer / anchor, the buyer / anchor generates a validated commercial transaction, and the capital provider extends financing on the strength of that visibility.

Supply Chain Finance and the Cash Conversion Cycle

Improving Liquidity Without Changing the Underlying Business Model

Supply Chain Finance can potentially affect the timing of cash flows. Consider:

Traditional Supplier Cycle

Production → Delivery → Invoice → 60–90 Day Wait → Payment

With Eligible Receivables Financing

Production → Delivery → Invoice → Transaction Validation → Earlier Liquidity → Financier Receives Payment at Maturity

The underlying commercial transaction may remain unchanged. What changes is the timing of liquidity availability. This can potentially help suppliers reduce dependence on unsecured borrowing, improve cash-flow visibility, support additional production and reduce liquidity stress.

Why Large Buyers Should Care

Supplier Liquidity Can Become a Procurement Risk

Supply Chain Finance is not only a supplier financing tool. It can also be a strategic treasury and procurement tool for large corporates. A financially stressed supplier ecosystem can lead to production disruption, vendor dependency, delayed deliveries and higher procurement costs.

An appropriately structured supplier finance program may support vendor stability, commercial continuity, supplier relationships and supply-chain resilience.

Dealer & Distributor Finance

Liquidity Challenges Do Not End With the Supplier

The downstream supply chain may also require financing. Consider: Manufacturer → Distributor → Dealer → Customer.

A dealer may need to purchase inventory before generating end-customer sales. This can create financing requirements around:

  • Stock acquisition
  • Seasonal demand
  • Dealer expansion
  • Distribution growth

Dealer finance structures can potentially use the commercial ecosystem to provide greater visibility into product movement, purchase patterns and sales cycles. The financing assessment remains transaction-specific.

Key Financial Metrics

Metrics that matter in supply chain liquidity:

Days Sales Outstanding (DSO)

Measures the average time required to collect receivables. Higher DSO can increase liquidity requirements.

Days Payable Outstanding (DPO)

Measures the average period taken to pay suppliers. Longer DPO may improve buyer liquidity but can increase pressure on suppliers.

Cash Conversion Cycle

Measures how long capital remains tied within the operating cycle. Supply Chain Finance may help optimise the liquidity timing for individual participants.

Receivables Ageing

Lenders assess current invoices, overdue invoices and ageing concentration. The age of the receivable can materially affect financing eligibility.

Dilution Rate

Measures historical reductions between invoiced value and actual cash collection. High dilution may indicate returns, disputes, credit notes or pricing adjustments.

Anchor Concentration

Measures dependence on a small number of buyers. A highly concentrated receivables book may be attractive if the anchor is strong — but concentration still requires underwriting.

When Supply Chain Finance May Not Be the Right Structure

Not Every Receivable Is Automatically Financeable

A receivable may be commercially valid but still be unsuitable for financing. Potential challenges can include:

  • High dispute rates
  • Long-overdue invoices
  • Unclear delivery evidence
  • Weak buyer credit
  • Excessive dilution
  • Concentrated customer risk
  • Poor transaction documentation

Similarly, Supply Chain Finance should not be used simply to mask structural liquidity problems.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Supply Chain Finance?

Supply Chain Finance refers to financing structures that improve liquidity across commercial relationships between buyers, suppliers, dealers and distributors. Rather than relying only on a single company's balance sheet, financing may also consider the quality of underlying transactions and payment relationships.

How is Supply Chain Finance different from Working Capital Finance?

Working Capital Finance primarily supports a company's own operating cycle through facilities such as Cash Credit, Overdraft and WCDL. Supply Chain Finance focuses more specifically on financing transactions and relationships across buyers, suppliers and distribution networks.

What is Reverse Factoring?

Reverse Factoring is a buyer-led financing structure in which suppliers may receive earlier payment against eligible obligations while the anchor buyer settles according to agreed commercial terms. The structure can leverage the credit quality of the buyer, subject to transaction-specific financing arrangements.

What is invoice discounting?

Invoice discounting involves converting eligible trade receivables into earlier liquidity. Financing eligibility depends on factors such as invoice validity, buyer quality, payment history, ageing and transaction documentation.

What is TReDS?

TReDS refers to the Trade Receivables Discounting System, a platform-based ecosystem designed to facilitate financing of eligible trade receivables through participating financiers. Eligibility and transaction processes depend on the applicable platform and regulatory framework.

What is the difference between recourse and non-recourse financing?

In recourse financing, the supplier may retain repayment obligations under defined circumstances. In non-recourse structures, financing risk allocation may be more closely linked to the buyer or underlying receivable, subject to transaction-specific terms and exclusions.

Can Supply Chain Finance help suppliers without reducing buyer payment terms?

Potentially, yes. A financing structure may allow an eligible supplier to access earlier liquidity while the buyer continues to operate under agreed commercial payment terms.

What do lenders evaluate in Supply Chain Finance?

Capital providers may evaluate anchor credit quality, invoice validation, historical payment behaviour, dispute and dilution rates, transaction tenor, concentration and the visibility of underlying commercial transactions.

Does EquiFin provide Supply Chain Finance directly?

No. EquiFin acts as an independent financial advisory firm supporting transaction structuring, financing strategy and capital-provider alignment. Financing decisions and terms remain subject to independent evaluation by relevant institutions.
