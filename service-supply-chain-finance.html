Supply Chain Finance

Liquidity Exists Across the Supply Chain. The Challenge Is Getting It to the Right Participant.

A supplier may have completed delivery but still be waiting for payment, while a distributor needs inventory financing before sales collections arrive. Supply Chain Finance structures liquidity around these commercial relationships — improving cash flow without treating every participant as an isolated borrower.

Where This Becomes Relevant

Supply chain liquidity problems tend to show up at specific points in the chain:

  • Suppliers Waiting Too Long for Payment — 60–120 day collection cycles while production and payroll still need funding.
  • Buyers Wanting to Support Vendors Without Paying Earlier — preserving payment terms while keeping the vendor base stable.
  • Dealers Needing Inventory Liquidity — stock purchases ahead of seasonal demand or distribution expansion.
  • Receivables Outgrowing Cash Generation — revenue-rich but liquidity-constrained as the book of credit sales expands.
  • MSME Suppliers With Limited Bank Access — where a credible buyer relationship (and platforms like TReDS) can open financing that standalone credit can’t.

What Capital Providers Evaluate

Supply Chain Finance lenders underwrite the transaction, not just the company’s financials:

  • Anchor Credit Quality — the buyer’s financial strength and payment behaviour, for anchor-led programs.
  • Invoice Validation — purchase order matching, delivery confirmation and digital verification.
  • Historical Payment Behaviour — actual payment days and default history, not just the balance sheet.
  • Disputes & Dilution — how much of invoiced value actually converts to cash after returns and adjustments.
  • Concentration — dependency on a small number of buyers or suppliers.
  • Transaction Tenor & Digital Visibility — whether the financing period matches the trade cycle, and how well the transaction can be tracked.

How the Financing Is Approached

A financing relationship here rests on three kinds of confidence: is the underlying transaction genuine, can the payment obligation be relied on, and can it be validated and monitored. We also work through whether the structure should be recourse (the supplier retains repayment risk) or non-recourse (risk sits closer to the buyer) — because that choice affects pricing, credit assessment and where the risk actually lands. Not every receivable is financeable: high dispute rates, weak buyer credit or poor documentation can rule a structure out regardless of how the transaction is packaged.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Supply Chain Finance?

Supply Chain Finance refers to financing structures that improve liquidity across commercial relationships between buyers, suppliers, dealers and distributors. Rather than relying only on a single company's balance sheet, financing may also consider the quality of underlying transactions and payment relationships.

What is TReDS?

TReDS refers to the Trade Receivables Discounting System, a platform-based ecosystem designed to facilitate financing of eligible trade receivables through participating financiers. Eligibility and transaction processes depend on the applicable platform and regulatory framework.

What is the difference between recourse and non-recourse financing?

In recourse financing, the supplier may retain repayment obligations under defined circumstances. In non-recourse structures, financing risk allocation may be more closely linked to the buyer or underlying receivable, subject to transaction-specific terms and exclusions.

Does EquiFin provide Supply Chain Finance directly?

No. EquiFin acts as an independent financial advisory firm supporting transaction structuring, financing strategy and capital-provider alignment. Financing decisions and terms remain subject to independent evaluation by relevant institutions.
