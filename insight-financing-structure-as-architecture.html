Beyond the Amount Raised: Financing Structure as a Portfolio of Obligations

A business can successfully raise the capital it needs and still end up with the wrong financing structure. The amount solves today’s requirement. The structure determines how comfortably the business can operate afterwards—and, as a company scales, that structure stops being a single transaction and becomes a portfolio of obligations.

10 Min Read·Perspective·September 2026

The Question

A company requires ₹200 crore. Management approaches lenders. After discussions, a financing proposal is received. The required amount is available. At first glance, the transaction appears successful. But an important question remains: is the financing actually structured in a way that works for the business? The amount of capital is only one dimension of a financing transaction — the same ₹200 crore can have very different consequences depending on tenor, moratorium, repayment profile, interest structure, security requirements, covenants, prepayment conditions and cash-flow alignment.

A financing transaction should therefore not be evaluated only through one question — how much capital have we raised? It should also be evaluated through another: what financial obligations and constraints have we created in the process of raising it? This distinction becomes particularly important for promoters and CFOs managing businesses through periods of expansion, investment and transformation, and for those who have already crossed the stage of simply “raising a loan” and are now thinking about the long-term consequences of capital decisions.

Capital Amount Solves the Immediate Problem. Structure Defines the Future.

Consider two businesses. Both raise the same amount of debt. Both receive capital at a broadly similar interest rate. But their financing structures differ. Company A has a shorter repayment period, immediate principal repayment, limited moratorium and a high annual debt-servicing burden. Company B has a longer repayment period, repayment aligned with project cash flows, an appropriate initial moratorium and greater flexibility during implementation. The amount raised may be identical — the financial experience of the two businesses may not be. This is why transaction structuring deserves attention before the financing is finalised, not after the funds are disbursed.

What Does “Financing Structure” Actually Mean?

Financing structure refers to how capital is designed around the underlying requirement. It may include several dimensions:

  • Amount
  • Tenor
  • Moratorium
  • Repayment profile
  • Interest structure
  • Security package
  • Covenants
  • Prepayment terms
  • Cash-flow alignment

Each element can affect the business differently. The objective is not necessarily to negotiate the most favourable term on every single parameter — it is to ensure the combined structure makes economic sense, reflecting the purpose of capital, the lifecycle of the underlying asset or project, the timing of cash generation, the risk profile of the business, and the company’s broader capital structure.

01 — Tenor Should Reflect the Economic Life of What Is Being Funded

One of the most fundamental structuring considerations is tenor. The question is simple: how long will the capital remain deployed before the underlying investment generates sufficient cash? Consider a company financing a long-term infrastructure asset that requires development time, construction, commissioning and operational stabilisation. If the financing tenure is significantly shorter than the economic cash-flow cycle, the company may experience repayment pressure before the asset has generated sufficient cash. The problem may not be the investment — it may be the mismatch between asset life and debt tenor, which can lead directly to early repayment pressure and refinancing or liquidity risk. Tenor is therefore not simply a negotiation point. It is part of the economics of the transaction.

02 — Repayment Should Follow Cash-Flow Reality

Debt repayment is often discussed in annual or quarterly schedules, but businesses do not always generate cash evenly. A project may take several years before becoming operational; a seasonal business may generate a significant proportion of cash during specific periods; a manufacturing expansion may require time before capacity utilisation stabilises. If repayment obligations begin before cash flows develop, financial pressure can emerge. A more appropriate structure considers when the business will actually have the capacity to service the debt — following capital deployment, through implementation, to when operations begin and cash flow stabilises, and only then to debt repayment. The closer the financing structure is aligned with the economic reality of the business, the more sustainable the transaction may be.

03 — A Moratorium Is About Timing, Not Free Capital

A moratorium is sometimes viewed simply as a concession, but its strategic purpose can be more important. During a development or implementation phase, the business may be deploying capital without yet generating operating cash. An appropriately structured moratorium can allow the project or investment to reach a stage where cash flows begin before significant principal repayment obligations arise. The relevant question is not whether the longest possible moratorium can be obtained — it is what period is realistically required before the underlying investment begins generating sufficient cash. Too short may create premature repayment pressure; too long may increase total interest costs or delay deleveraging unnecessarily. The appropriate structure should reflect the actual business timeline.

04 — Repayment Profile Matters More Than a Single Tenor Number

Two loans may both have a seven-year tenor, but their repayment profiles may differ significantly — equal principal repayment from the first year, an initial moratorium followed by gradual repayment, or lower initial repayment with higher repayments later. The headline tenor is identical; the cash-flow burden is not. This is why promoters and CFOs should examine the full debt service schedule year by year and compare it against projected operating cash flow for each period. The transaction should not be assessed only on whether the total debt can theoretically be repaid — it should consider whether the business can meet each repayment obligation when it actually falls due.

05 — Security Structure Can Affect Future Financial Flexibility

Security is often treated as a standard part of financing, but security decisions can have long-term implications. A lender may seek security over fixed assets, current assets, project assets, receivables, shares or corporate guarantees. The issue is not simply whether the business can provide security today — management should also consider what assets and flexibility will remain available for future financing. A company may have significant borrowing capacity today, but if all key assets are comprehensively encumbered, future transactions may become more complex. This is particularly relevant for businesses planning future capex, acquisitions, new projects, additional working capital or refinancing — security planning should be connected to the broader capital roadmap.

06 — Covenants Can Shape Management Flexibility

Financial covenants are another important dimension of financing structure, relating to leverage, debt service coverage, liquidity, additional borrowing, asset disposals or dividend distribution. Covenants are not necessarily problematic — they are often part of institutional lending discipline. But management should understand their operational implications: a covenant may appear manageable under the base case, but what happens if revenue growth slows, margins decline, a major investment is required, or working capital increases? The key question is how much operating and strategic flexibility remains within the financing framework, evaluated not only at the date of signing but across realistic future scenarios.

07 — Interest Cost Is Important, But Not the Only Cost

Borrowers naturally focus on interest rates, and this is reasonable — interest cost directly affects profitability and cash flows. But a financing transaction has other economic consequences. A lower interest rate with a shorter tenor, higher repayment burden and restrictive covenants may create more operational pressure than a slightly higher-cost structure with better cash-flow alignment, a longer repayment profile and greater flexibility. This does not mean the highest-cost financing is preferable — it means interest rate should be considered alongside the complete structure. The effective economic question is what the overall cost of living with this capital will be, including both financial expense and strategic constraints.

08 — Prepayment Terms Matter When the Business Performs Better

A financing transaction is often structured based on current expectations, but businesses evolve. Suppose cash flows improve significantly, a major asset is sold, equity is raised, or refinancing becomes available — management may want to reduce or replace existing debt. Prepayment conditions can therefore become relevant, and the financing documentation may address voluntary prepayment, mandatory prepayment, prepayment charges and refinancing conditions. These may not be central to the initial decision, but they can become important later. A good financing structure should consider not only how capital enters the business, but also how the business may eventually reduce or exit that capital.

09 — Financing Structure Should Consider the Downside Case

A transaction may look comfortable under the base case — revenue rising, margins stable, collections normal. The more important question is what happens under a downside case, where revenue falls, margins decline, collections slow and costs rise. Does the business still have liquidity headroom, covenant capacity, debt servicing ability and operational flexibility? The objective is not to structure financing around an extreme worst-case scenario, but reasonable downside resilience should be considered — a capital structure that works only under perfect execution may carry hidden risk.

A Financing Transaction Is a Portfolio of Obligations

When a company raises multiple facilities over time, management can sometimes evaluate each transaction separately. But collectively, these facilities create a portfolio of obligations — a term loan, working capital, project debt, lease obligations and other facilities together form the total financial obligation. Each facility may appear manageable individually, but the combined structure may create repayment concentration, security overlap, covenant complexity and liquidity pressure. This is why CFOs should periodically look at the complete financing architecture. The relevant question is not whether each individual facility is acceptable — it is whether the combined capital structure remains manageable.

Structure Becomes More Important as Businesses Scale

A small business may initially rely on a limited number of financing relationships. As the company grows, capital requirements become more complex — it may simultaneously require working capital, capex financing, project funding, acquisition financing and structured facilities. At this stage, financing becomes an architecture problem: the business needs to consider how multiple sources of capital interact, from business strategy through capital requirements, multiple financing sources and combined financial obligations, to capital structure and, ultimately, future flexibility. The objective is not simply to close transactions — it is to ensure that each transaction contributes positively to the overall financial architecture.

EquiFin Perspective

The amount of capital determines whether a transaction can fund the immediate requirement. The structure determines whether that capital remains sustainable throughout the life of the business plan.

Key Takeaways

  • Raising the required amount of capital does not automatically mean the financing transaction is optimally structured.
  • Tenor should be evaluated against the economic life and cash-flow cycle of the underlying investment.
  • Repayment profiles matter as much as headline loan maturity.
  • Moratorium periods should reflect actual implementation and cash-generation timelines.
  • Security commitments can influence future borrowing flexibility.
  • Financial covenants should be evaluated under realistic downside scenarios.
  • Multiple facilities should be evaluated collectively as part of a broader capital architecture.
  • The best financing structure is one that aligns capital obligations with business reality.

Closing Perspective

When a company needs capital, the immediate objective is naturally to secure funding. But financing decisions continue to affect the business long after the transaction closes — every facility creates repayment obligations, security commitments, financial constraints and future opportunities.

That is why successful financing should not be defined only by the amount raised. A better definition is: has the business raised the right amount of capital, in a structure that its cash flows and strategy can realistically support? That is the difference between arranging funding and structuring capital — and as businesses become larger and more complex, that difference becomes increasingly important.
