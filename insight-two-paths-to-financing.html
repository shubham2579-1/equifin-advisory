Two Paths to Financing: Corporate Debt or Project Finance?

Not every large capital requirement should be financed through the corporate balance sheet. The right financing approach depends on where the cash flows will come from, how risks are allocated and whether the underlying project can stand on its own financial economics.

7 Min Read·Perspective·September 2026

The Question

A company plans a major investment — a manufacturing facility, an infrastructure asset, a data centre, a hospitality development. The financing decision: borrow against the existing corporate balance sheet, or structure and finance the project independently? Both provide capital, both involve lenders, both require repayment — but the underlying credit approach differs fundamentally, centred on one question: is the lender relying on the company's overall balance sheet, or on the future economics of the specific project?

Corporate debt evaluates the borrowing company itself — historical performance, existing cash flows, leverage, management track record — and works well when the company has established operations and predictable cash flows. Project finance evaluates the project directly — cost, implementation plan, revenue model, contracts, cash flows — often through a dedicated SPV holding the project's assets. Not every investment suits standalone project financing: a small production line added inside an existing facility has cash flows fully integrated into the business, so corporate financing is more practical; a large renewable energy project with defined capital cost, separate contracts and identifiable revenues has a distinct enough economic identity to make a project structure more relevant.

Two Paths to Financing

The question is not whether project finance is better than corporate debt. It is which financing structure best reflects the economic reality of the investment.

What Actually Separates the Two

  • The source of repayment is the central difference. Corporate debt traces back to overall company cash flows; project finance traces back to the project's own revenues — which is why a project lender needs confidence in the complete project economics, not just the sponsor.
  • Project finance needs deeper visibility into the project itself — land and approvals in development, contractor capability in construction, technical assumptions in operations, contracts and tariffs in revenue. A weakness in any one area can affect the whole financing outcome.
  • Risk allocation gets far more explicit. Who bears cost overruns, who bears a demand shortfall, who's responsible after commissioning — project finance distributes each risk between participants rather than folding it into one general corporate risk assessment.
  • Corporate debt can pressure the balance sheet in ways a project structure sometimes avoids — though project finance doesn't remove all sponsor exposure; equity commitments, completion support and guarantees are often still required.
  • Project finance demands much more upfront preparation — a coherent financial model connecting project cost, construction timeline, revenue assumptions and debt servicing, since there's no existing financial history to lean on.
  • Cash-flow timing determines debt capacity as much as eventual profitability does — a two-year construction period with a year to stabilise means repayment has to be sequenced against when cash actually starts arriving, not before.
  • Project finance isn't only for massive projects. A smaller project with clear, contracted cash flows can be easier to finance this way than a much larger one with uncertain economics — size matters less than how clearly the project stands on its own.
  • Corporate debt tends to fit when the investment is tightly integrated into existing operations and cash flows are already strong enough to absorb it, with no standalone contractual revenue to point to instead.
  • Project finance tends to fit when the assets, capital cost and revenue model can all be identified independently and the project has a long enough economic life to support its own debt servicing.
  • Hybrid structures are common in practice — a corporate loan through development, project debt after commissioning, sponsor support during construction — reflecting how a project's risk profile changes as it moves from concept to operations.

EquiFin Perspective

The choice between corporate debt and project finance should begin with the economics of repayment. The structure should reflect where cash will be generated, when it will become available and which risks can affect its predictability.

Key Takeaways

  • Corporate debt and project finance are based on different credit perspectives.
  • Corporate debt primarily relies on the overall company’s financial strength and cash flows.
  • Project finance places greater emphasis on the economics and cash flows of a specific project.
  • A project needs a sufficiently identifiable economic structure to support standalone financing analysis.
  • The primary source of repayment is central to determining the appropriate financing approach.
  • Cash-flow timing is particularly important for projects with long development or implementation periods.
  • Hybrid structures may be appropriate where the risk profile changes during the lifecycle of a project.
  • The financing product should follow the economics of the transaction—not precede them.

Closing Perspective

An established company expanding an existing operation may be appropriately financed through corporate borrowing. A long-life infrastructure or renewable energy asset with defined cash flows may need a more specialised project financing approach. Some transactions need a combination of both. What matters is the alignment between capital requirement, asset economics, cash-flow timing, risk allocation and debt servicing — the right structure is ultimately the one that understands where the investment creates value, and designs capital around that journey.
