Two Paths to Financing: Corporate Debt or Project Finance?

Not every large capital requirement should be financed through the corporate balance sheet. The right financing approach depends on where the cash flows will come from, how risks are allocated and whether the underlying project can stand on its own financial economics.

11 Min Read·Perspective·September 2026

The Question

A company is planning a major investment — a new manufacturing facility, an infrastructure asset, a renewable energy project, a logistics park, a data centre, a large hospitality development or a specialised industrial facility. The management team now faces an important financing decision: should the company borrow against its existing corporate balance sheet, or should the project itself be structured and financed independently?

At first glance, both approaches appear to solve the same problem — both provide capital, both involve lenders, both require repayment. But the underlying credit approach can be fundamentally different. The distinction is centred around one question: is the lender primarily relying on the existing company’s overall balance sheet and cash flows, or on the future economics and cash flows of the specific project being financed? That difference influences almost every part of the transaction.

What Is Corporate Debt?

Corporate debt is financing provided primarily based on the credit profile of the borrowing company. The lender evaluates factors such as historical financial performance, existing cash flows, profitability, net worth, leverage, working capital behaviour and management track record. The company may use the capital for capex, expansion, acquisition, refinancing or general corporate purposes, and the source of repayment is generally linked to the company’s overall cash generation. This structure can work effectively when the company has established operations, predictable cash flows, strong financial performance and adequate borrowing capacity.

What Is Project Finance?

Project finance focuses more specifically on the economics of a defined project. The lender analyses project cost, implementation plan, revenue model, operating assumptions, project cash flows, contractual framework and risk allocation — often through a dedicated project SPV holding the project assets, contracts, revenues and cash flows. The key difference is the greater focus on whether the project itself can generate sufficient cash flow to support the financing, which requires deeper analysis of project economics.

The First Question: Does the Project Have an Independent Economic Life?

Not every investment is suitable for standalone project financing. A useful starting question is whether the project can be identified as a distinct economic unit. Consider a company adding a small production line inside an existing manufacturing facility — the incremental revenue may be difficult to isolate, the operating infrastructure may be shared, and the cash flows may be fully integrated into the existing business, so corporate financing may be more practical. Now consider a large renewable energy project with defined capital cost, specific assets, separate contracts, identifiable revenues and long-term operating assumptions — this has a more distinct economic identity, which may make a project-oriented financing structure more relevant.

Two Paths to Financing

The question is not whether project finance is better than corporate debt. It is which financing structure best reflects the economic reality of the investment.

01 — The Source of Repayment Is the Central Difference

Every financing transaction eventually returns to one fundamental question: where will the money come from to repay the debt? Under corporate debt, the path runs from total company operations through overall corporate cash flows to interest and principal. Under project finance, the path runs from project development through project operations, project revenues and project cash flows, to interest and principal. This creates a significant difference in underwriting — the project finance lender needs confidence not only in the sponsor, but in the complete project economics.

02 — Project Finance Requires Greater Visibility Into the Project

A corporate lender may evaluate the overall business. A project finance lender needs to understand the project at a deeper level — land availability, approvals and project readiness in development; cost estimates, contractor capability and implementation schedule in construction; operating costs, technical assumptions and maintenance requirements in operations; demand assumptions, contracts, tariff structures and customer arrangements in revenue; and capital structure, debt servicing and sensitivity analysis in finance. The project is effectively analysed as a financial ecosystem, and a weakness in one area can affect the overall financing outcome.

03 — Risk Allocation Becomes More Important

Corporate lending primarily evaluates whether the company can manage business risks. Project finance requires greater attention to who is carrying each specific project risk — who is responsible if construction costs increase or timelines are delayed, what happens if actual utilisation is below expectations, who manages the asset after commissioning, what approvals or regulatory dependencies exist, and who is responsible for making payments to the project. A project finance structure often examines how construction, demand, operating, regulatory and counterparty risks are distributed between participants. The objective is not to eliminate every risk — that is rarely possible. It is to understand and allocate risks appropriately.

04 — Corporate Debt Can Create Balance Sheet Pressure

A company with a strong existing balance sheet may use corporate borrowing to finance a new investment — straightforward, but a large project can significantly increase corporate leverage, raising future borrowing capacity concerns, financial ratio pressure, covenant headroom and liquidity flexibility. A project-oriented structure may sometimes allow the investment to be evaluated more independently. However, this does not automatically mean project finance removes all sponsor exposure — depending on the transaction, sponsors may still provide equity commitments, completion support, guarantees or other contractual support. The appropriate structure depends on the transaction.

05 — Project Finance Requires Stronger Upfront Preparation

Corporate financing can often rely substantially on the company’s existing financial history. Project finance requires a detailed view of the future, which creates a greater preparation requirement — moving from project concept through feasibility, cost estimation, technical assessment, revenue model, financial model, risk analysis and financing structure. The financial model becomes particularly important, connecting project cost, construction timeline, revenue assumptions, operating costs, debt servicing and equity returns. Without a coherent project model, it becomes difficult to evaluate financing capacity.

06 — Cash Flow Timing Determines Debt Capacity

A project may ultimately be profitable, but project finance also depends on timing. Consider a project requiring two years of construction and one year of operational stabilisation — it may not generate meaningful operating cash flow for several years, and debt servicing must account for this, moving from development through construction, commissioning, operations, cash-flow generation and only then debt repayment. If repayment begins significantly before cash generation, the project may face avoidable pressure, which is one reason project financing requires close alignment between construction schedule, commissioning, revenue generation and debt servicing.

07 — Project Finance Is Not Only About Large Projects

Project finance is sometimes associated exclusively with massive infrastructure developments, but the core principle is broader. The relevance of a project-oriented financing structure depends less on project size alone and more on defined project economics, identifiable assets, predictable cash flows, contractual visibility and risk allocation. A relatively smaller project with strong cash-flow visibility may be easier to evaluate than a much larger project with uncertain economics. The question is not how large the project is — it is how clearly the project can be understood as an independent economic and financial proposition.

08 — When Corporate Debt May Be More Appropriate

Corporate debt can be particularly relevant when the investment is integrated into existing operations and cannot easily be separated from the current business, when the company already has strong cash flows that can comfortably support additional borrowing, when the capital requirement is relatively straightforward (such as standard capex or business expansion), and when the project does not have standalone contractual cash flows, so the business itself remains the primary source of repayment.

09 — When Project Finance May Become More Relevant

Project finance may become more relevant when the project has identifiable assets, when the capital cost can be independently determined, when the revenue model can be separately analysed, when the project has a long-term economic life, when project cash flows can support debt servicing, and when risks can be identified and allocated. Financing suitability ultimately depends on the complete transaction context—not any single factor in isolation.

10 — Hybrid Structures Are Also Possible

In practice, financing is not always purely corporate debt or purely project finance. Transactions may include combinations such as a corporate loan during development, project debt after commissioning, sponsor support during construction, limited recourse structures, or refinancing after operational stabilisation — moving from the development phase and sponsor or corporate support, through project implementation, to operations stabilising, project cash flows developing, and eventually long-term project financing. This can sometimes reflect the changing risk profile of an asset as the financing structure evolves alongside the project itself.

The Most Important Decision: Understand the Financing Requirement Before Choosing the Product

Companies sometimes begin with a product-oriented approach — “we need project finance” or “we need a corporate term loan.” A more effective starting point may be to ask what exactly is being funded, how much capital is required, when cash flows will emerge, what the key risks are, whether the investment can be evaluated independently, and what repayment structure the economics can support. Only after these questions are answered should the financing approach be determined — moving from business requirement through capital requirement, cash-flow analysis, risk analysis and repayment capacity, to financing structure. The financing product should follow the economics. Not the other way around.

EquiFin Perspective

The choice between corporate debt and project finance should begin with the economics of repayment. The structure should reflect where cash will be generated, when it will become available and which risks can affect its predictability.

Key Takeaways

  • Corporate debt and project finance are based on different credit perspectives.
  • Corporate debt primarily relies on the overall company’s financial strength and cash flows.
  • Project finance places greater emphasis on the economics and cash flows of a specific project.
  • A project needs a sufficiently identifiable economic structure to support standalone financing analysis.
  • The primary source of repayment is central to determining the appropriate financing approach.
  • Cash-flow timing is particularly important for projects with long development or implementation periods.
  • Hybrid structures may be appropriate where the risk profile changes during the lifecycle of a project.
  • The financing product should follow the economics of the transaction—not precede them.

Closing Perspective

An established company expanding an existing operation may be appropriately financed through corporate borrowing. A long-life infrastructure or renewable energy asset with defined cash flows may require a more specialised project financing approach. Some transactions may require a combination of both.

What matters is the alignment between capital requirement, asset economics, cash-flow timing, risk allocation and debt servicing. The right financing structure is ultimately the one that understands where the investment creates value—and designs capital around that journey.
