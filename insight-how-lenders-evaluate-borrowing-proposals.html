How Lenders Actually Evaluate a Corporate Borrowing Proposal

A corporate borrowing proposal may begin with the amount of capital required. Institutional evaluation, however, usually begins with a different set of questions: why is the capital needed, how will it be used, and what gives the lender confidence that the obligation can be serviced?

7 Min Read·Perspective·September 2026

The Question Behind the Question

A company may approach a lender with what looks like a straightforward case — growing revenue, available security, a clear expansion plan. From the borrower's side, the transaction looks compelling. The lender's response can still be more cautious than expected, because a lender isn't evaluating the amount requested. It's evaluating what the business does, why the capital is needed, where future cash flows will come from, what obligations already exist, and what happens if the assumptions don't hold.

"We need ₹200 crore for expansion" describes a requirement. Explaining that capacity is expanding against demonstrated demand, over a defined timeline, with cash flows supporting the proposed repayment — that describes a transaction. Lenders evaluate the second, not the first. Their central question is the probability that capital gets repaid on the agreed terms, not the upside case an equity investor looks for — which is why no single ratio usually explains a credit decision on its own.

What Lenders Actually Look At

  • The business itself. Not just what it sells, but the economic model behind the cash flows — customers, concentration, demand drivers, competitive position. Two companies can report similar revenue and EBITDA and still carry very different risk, depending on whether that performance rests on long-term contracts and diversified demand, or a handful of customers and cyclical pricing.
  • Management and promoter track record. Execution history, governance, financial discipline, and — for promoter-led businesses especially — whether what management says the business will do lines up with what it has actually delivered through past cycles.
  • Historical performance, read for quality, not just growth. Why margins moved, why receivables grew faster than revenue, why debt rose despite improving profitability — the goal is understanding the story behind the changes, not just spotting positive numbers.
  • Cash flow, separately from profit. A profitable company can still be cash-constrained if receivables, inventory, capex and existing debt absorb most of what EBITDA generates — which matters most exactly when evaluating incremental debt.
  • Existing debt and its maturity profile. Not just how much debt exists, but whether repayments are concentrated in the next two years, what's already charged, and whether existing covenants constrain fresh borrowing.
  • Why the capital is needed. Working capital, capacity expansion, an acquisition and a project each get underwritten differently — even at an identical loan amount.
  • Debt-servicing capacity, and the assumptions behind it. Coverage ratios are tools, not answers — a model can produce attractive ratios that are only as reliable as the growth and margin assumptions feeding them.
  • Sector context. The same financial profile reads differently in real estate, infrastructure or manufacturing — financial analysis without industry context misses real underwriting considerations.
  • Security and transaction structure. Security protects the downside; cash flow services the debt day to day — and for the same amount, tenor, repayment schedule and covenants can materially change how a transaction is received.
  • Downside scenarios. What happens if revenue, margins or timelines don't play out as planned — the strongest structures are the ones built to stay resilient, not the ones built around the best case.

Preparation Changes the Conversation

Many financing discussions stall because the transaction is presented before it's fully prepared — the requirement isn't quantified precisely, the financial model isn't reconciled, or key risks haven't been identified. Addressing these before approaching a lender doesn't guarantee financing, but it materially improves the quality of the conversation.

EquiFin Perspective

A strong borrowing proposal does not simply explain how much capital a business requires. It connects the business model, financial performance, use of funds, future cash flows and transaction structure into one coherent credit narrative.

Key Takeaways

  • A borrowing proposal is more than a request for a specific amount of capital.
  • Lenders generally evaluate the relationship between the business, use of funds, cash flows and proposed repayment obligations.
  • Historical profitability is important, but cash-flow generation and debt servicing capacity require separate analysis.
  • Existing debt, maturity concentration and security utilisation can materially influence incremental borrowing capacity.
  • The underlying purpose of capital affects how a transaction may be evaluated and structured.
  • Financial projections are most useful when their underlying assumptions can be clearly explained.
  • Transaction structure—including tenor, repayment and security—can materially influence the suitability of a financing proposal.
  • Preparation is not a guarantee of financing, but a well-prepared transaction is easier for institutions to understand and evaluate.

Closing Perspective

A lender's process can look complex from outside — credit committees, models, documentation, internal approvals. The underlying objective is simpler: the institution is trying to work out whether the business and the proposed transaction create an acceptable relationship between risk and repayment capacity. For promoters and CFOs, that means the financing process shouldn't begin only with how much capital can be raised. It should also begin with what an institution would need to understand to get comfortable — thinking that through before approaching the market usually improves the conversation considerably.
