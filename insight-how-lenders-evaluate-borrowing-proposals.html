How Lenders Actually Evaluate a Corporate Borrowing Proposal

A corporate borrowing proposal may begin with the amount of capital required. Institutional evaluation, however, usually begins with a different set of questions: why is the capital needed, how will it be used, and what gives the lender confidence that the obligation can be serviced?

10 Min Read·Perspective·September 2026

The Question

A company may approach a lender with what appears to be a straightforward requirement. The business is growing, financial performance is improving, assets are available as security, and the management team has a clear plan for expansion. From the borrower’s perspective, the transaction may appear compelling. Yet the lender’s response can be more cautious than expected.

Why? Because a lender does not evaluate only the amount being requested. A corporate borrowing proposal is generally assessed through multiple interconnected questions — what does the business actually do, why is capital required, how has the company performed historically, where will future cash flows come from, what existing financial obligations already exist, how will the proposed debt be serviced, what happens if assumptions do not materialise, and what is the lender’s downside protection? The financing requirement is therefore only the starting point. The real evaluation begins with understanding the business and the transaction behind the requirement.

A Borrowing Proposal Is Not Just a Funding Request

Businesses often prepare for financing discussions by focusing heavily on the amount of capital required — for example, “the company requires ₹200 crore for expansion.” But from a credit perspective, the number itself does not provide enough context. The lender needs to understand what sits behind it. A more complete transaction narrative explains that the company is expanding manufacturing capacity to address demonstrated demand, that capital expenditure will be deployed over a defined implementation period, and that the expanded facility is expected to begin commercial operations within a specified timeline, with projected cash flows supporting the proposed repayment structure. The first statement describes a requirement. The second explains a transaction — and lenders generally evaluate the relationship between the business, the use of funds and the proposed repayment capacity.

The Lender Is Asking a Different Question

A promoter may ask, “can we raise ₹200 crore?” A CFO may ask, “what will the cost of borrowing be?” A lender may begin somewhere else: what is the probability that this capital will be repaid according to the agreed terms? That fundamental difference shapes the evaluation process. The lender is not participating in the transaction as an equity investor and generally does not benefit proportionately from unlimited upside — its primary concern is the ability of the borrower to meet contractual obligations. A credit evaluation therefore often moves through business quality, financial performance, cash-flow generation, debt servicing capacity, transaction structure and downside protection. Each element interacts with the others: a strong business may still have weak cash conversion; strong assets may not compensate for an unsustainable repayment profile; high EBITDA may not translate into sufficient cash for debt servicing. This is why no single metric usually explains the entire credit decision.

01 — Understanding the Underlying Business

Before evaluating ratios and financial models, a lender needs to understand the underlying business — not simply the company’s products or services, but the economic model that generates its cash flows. Questions may include what the company sells, who its customers are, how concentrated the customer base is, what drives demand, how competitive the industry is, what differentiates the business, how predictable revenues are and what the key operating risks are. Two companies may report similar revenues and EBITDA yet have materially different credit profiles — one with long-term customer contracts, diversified revenue, predictable margins and established operating history; the other dependent on a small number of customers, cyclical demand, volatile raw material prices and short-term contracts. The financial statements may look similar. The underlying risk can be very different, which is why a financing proposal should explain the business model behind the numbers.

02 — Management and Promoter Perspective

Corporate lending is not only an assessment of assets and financial statements. The management team and promoter group can also be an important part of the institutional evaluation — capital providers may seek to understand management experience, execution track record, strategic clarity, governance practices, financial discipline and the ability to navigate business cycles. This does not mean a lender expects every promoter to have a flawless history; businesses operate through changing markets and cycles. The relevant consideration is often how management has responded to challenges and how clearly the company communicates its strategic position. For promoter-led businesses in India, institutional confidence can also be influenced by the consistency between what management says the business will do and what the business has historically demonstrated it can execute.

03 — Historical Financial Performance

Historical financial statements provide important context, but the purpose is not simply to observe whether revenue and profit have increased. A lender may examine the quality and sustainability of performance — revenue growth trends, customer concentration, diversification and stability; EBITDA margins, margin trends, operating efficiency and sensitivity to input costs; the strength of the capital base, retained earnings and equity support; and operating cash generation, working capital absorption and capital expenditure requirements. The lender may also seek to understand variations — why margins improved significantly, why receivables increased faster than revenue, or why debt increased despite improved profitability. The objective is not simply to identify positive numbers; it is to understand the story behind changes in those numbers.

04 — Cash Flow Often Matters More Than the Headline Profit Number

A profitable company can still experience financial pressure when cash is absorbed by receivables, inventory, capital expenditure, taxes and existing debt obligations. The question is not only whether the company is profitable — it is also how much cash is actually available after meeting the requirements necessary to operate and grow the business. This distinction becomes particularly important when evaluating incremental debt: a company may generate strong EBITDA while simultaneously requiring significant capital for growth, and if most internally generated cash is reinvested into inventory, receivables, new capacity or projects, the amount available for debt servicing may be lower than headline profitability suggests.

05 — Existing Debt Changes the Conversation

No incremental borrowing proposal should be evaluated in isolation. A lender will generally want to understand the company’s existing financial obligations — term loans, working capital facilities, NBFC borrowings, lease obligations, external commercial borrowings, promoter guarantees and group-level obligations where relevant. The key consideration is not only how much debt the company currently has, but what that debt looks like: whether significant repayments are concentrated over the next two years, whether obligations are evenly distributed or heavily front-loaded, which assets are already charged, whether the existing cost of debt is sustainable, and whether covenants could affect incremental borrowing. A company may have manageable total debt but a difficult maturity profile; conversely, a company with significant debt may have manageable obligations if maturities and cash flows are appropriately aligned. Context matters.

06 — Why the Capital Is Required

The use of funds is central to transaction evaluation, and a lender may view different uses of capital differently. For working capital, the focus may be on the operating cycle, receivable quality, inventory movement and customer concentration. For manufacturing expansion, it may include capacity utilisation, demand visibility, implementation timeline and asset life. For acquisition finance, the evaluation may consider strategic rationale, acquisition valuation, integration risk and post-transaction leverage. For project finance, the focus may include project economics, contracts, implementation risk and cash-flow visibility. The amount of financing may be identical across these situations — the underwriting logic can be very different, which is why a transaction proposal should explain not only the use of funds but also the economic rationale behind the investment.

07 — The Ability to Service Debt

Ultimately, debt needs to be serviced — this is where financial analysis moves from historical performance to future capacity. Lenders may examine projected revenue, EBITDA, operating cash flows, interest obligations, principal repayment, capital expenditure and working capital requirements, using measures such as debt service coverage, interest coverage, leverage, fixed charge coverage or cash-flow coverage depending on the nature of the business. However, ratios are only tools — the more important question is whether the underlying assumptions are realistic. A financial model can produce attractive ratios, but the model is only as reliable as the assumptions supporting it.

08 — Lenders Also Evaluate the Assumptions

Financial projections are an important part of many financing proposals, but lenders generally do not accept projections without examining the assumptions behind them — what supports projected revenue growth, whether margins are sustainable, whether the business can operationally deliver the projected growth, whether additional growth will require additional funding, whether all implementation costs have been considered, and what happens if the project is delayed. This does not mean projections need to be pessimistic. It means they should be explainable — a strong financial model is not necessarily the one showing the highest growth, but the one where management can clearly explain why the assumptions are reasonable.

09 — Sector Conditions Matter

A company does not operate independently from its sector. The same financial profile may be evaluated differently depending on industry cyclicality, regulatory exposure, competitive intensity, demand stability, commodity sensitivity and technological disruption. Lenders may examine project approvals, sales velocity, development risk and cash-flow timing in real estate; contractual framework, project execution and counterparty quality in infrastructure; capacity utilisation, input costs and customer concentration in manufacturing; regulatory requirements, occupancy and operating margins in healthcare; and generation assumptions, contract structure and counterparty exposure in renewable energy. This is why financing transactions benefit from sector-specific context — financial analysis without industry context can miss important underwriting considerations.

10 — Security Matters, But It Is Not the Entire Credit Story

Security provides lenders with an additional layer of protection, which may include property, plant and machinery, receivables, financial assets, project assets, corporate guarantees and personal guarantees where applicable. However, security alone does not necessarily answer the central credit question — an asset may have significant value, but if the business cannot generate sufficient cash to meet ongoing obligations, the transaction may still present challenges. A useful distinction: security can provide protection against downside, while cash flow generally supports ongoing repayment. Both may be important, but their roles are different.

11 — The Structure of the Transaction Can Change the Credit Outcome

Two financing proposals for the same company may receive different responses depending on how they are structured. Consider a company seeking ₹100 crore — one structure with a short tenor, immediate principal repayment, significant security requirement and limited flexibility; another with tenor aligned with asset life, repayment aligned with projected cash flows, clearly defined use of funds and an appropriate security structure. The underlying company has not changed, but the transaction structure has. This is why financing is not only about accessing capital — it is also about structuring the transaction to reflect the economic characteristics of the requirement, across elements such as amount, tenor, moratorium, repayment schedule, security, covenants, equity contribution and conditions precedent. The objective is not to optimise one element in isolation, but to create a structure that works across the transaction.

12 — Downside Scenarios Are Part of Credit Thinking

Promoters generally build businesses around opportunities. Credit evaluation also requires thinking about what could go wrong — revenue growth below expectations, margin compression, delayed project completion, higher interest rates, slower collections and cost overruns. The objective is not to assume every negative scenario will occur, but to understand the resilience of the capital structure if conditions become less favourable. A strong transaction should be capable of answering what happens if the base case does not unfold exactly as expected — which is where sensitivity analysis becomes useful. The strongest financing structures are not necessarily those designed around the most optimistic scenario; they are structures that retain reasonable resilience when assumptions change.

The Importance of Transaction Preparation

Many financing discussions become difficult because the transaction is presented before it is fully prepared. The business may know it needs capital, but important questions may still be unresolved — has the capital requirement been clearly quantified, is the use of funds fully defined, are historical financials reconciled, does the financial model reflect realistic assumptions, is the debt requirement aligned with projected cash flows, have existing obligations been mapped, and have key transaction risks been identified? When these questions are addressed early, the institutional conversation can become more focused. Preparation does not guarantee financing, but it can improve the quality of the transaction being presented.

Can we clearly explain why this capital is required?

Can we demonstrate how the capital will be deployed?

04

How will the proposed financing be serviced?

05

What does our existing debt maturity profile look like?

How much cash is absorbed by working capital and capex?

07

Which assumptions drive our financial projections?

08

What are the most important risks to those assumptions?

09

Does the proposed financing structure align with the purpose of capital?

10

Can we explain the transaction from a lender’s perspective—not only our own?

EquiFin Perspective

A strong borrowing proposal does not simply explain how much capital a business requires. It connects the business model, financial performance, use of funds, future cash flows and transaction structure into one coherent credit narrative.

Key Takeaways

  • A borrowing proposal is more than a request for a specific amount of capital.
  • Lenders generally evaluate the relationship between the business, use of funds, cash flows and proposed repayment obligations.
  • Historical profitability is important, but cash-flow generation and debt servicing capacity require separate analysis.
  • Existing debt, maturity concentration and security utilisation can materially influence incremental borrowing capacity.
  • The underlying purpose of capital affects how a transaction may be evaluated and structured.
  • Financial projections are most useful when their underlying assumptions can be clearly explained.
  • Transaction structure—including tenor, repayment and security—can materially influence the suitability of a financing proposal.
  • Preparation is not a guarantee of financing, but a well-prepared transaction is easier for institutions to understand and evaluate.

Closing Perspective

A lender’s decision-making process may appear complex from outside — there are credit committees, financial models, documentation, industry analysis and internal processes. But the fundamental objective is straightforward: the institution is trying to understand whether the business and the proposed transaction create an acceptable relationship between risk and repayment capacity.

For promoters and CFOs, the implication is important. The financing process should not begin only with how much capital can be raised — it should also begin with what an institution would need to understand in order to become comfortable with this transaction. Thinking through that question before approaching the market can materially improve the quality of the financing conversation.
