Corporate Finance

Capital Structures Should Support Growth. Not Constrain It.

As businesses grow, financing decisions become more complex than simply raising additional debt. Expansion capital, acquisitions, refinancing and balance-sheet optimisation require a structure that considers leverage, repayment capacity, security, cost of capital and the long-term flexibility of the business.

EquiFin helps promoters and CFOs assess these trade-offs, structure appropriate capital solutions and manage the transaction through lender evaluation to financial close.

Corporate Finance Is About More Than Raising Capital

A business may have access to debt and still have an inefficient capital structure. The issue is often not the availability of financing, but whether the proposed borrowing matches the purpose of the transaction, the company's operating cash flows and its ability to absorb additional leverage.

Corporate finance decisions become particularly important when a company is entering a new phase — expanding capacity, acquiring another business, refinancing legacy borrowings or consolidating fragmented banking arrangements. At this stage, the questions become more strategic:

  • How much additional debt can the business prudently carry?
  • Should growth be financed through senior debt, structured debt or promoter capital?
  • Is the repayment profile aligned with cash generation?
  • Are existing facilities restricting future borrowing flexibility?
  • Can refinancing improve maturity, security or covenant terms?
  • Is the business approaching lenders with a structure that fits their credit appetite?

EquiFin approaches corporate finance as a capital-structure and transaction-execution exercise, not as a product distribution exercise.

The Right Capital Depends on What the Business Is Trying to Achieve

Corporate capital requirements evolve with the business. A company in a stable operating phase may prioritise refinancing and cost optimisation. A growing company may require long-term capital without prematurely diluting promoter ownership. An acquisition may require a different financing structure altogether, particularly when the acquisition entity, operating company and security pool are not aligned. The capital structure should therefore begin with the transaction.

Growth & Expansion — Funding new capacity, geographic expansion, product lines or strategic investments.

Acquisition Financing — Structuring debt for acquisitions, promoter-led buyouts or strategic consolidation.

Refinancing — Replacing existing facilities to improve maturity profiles, repayment terms or overall financing flexibility.

Structured Corporate Debt — Addressing capital requirements that may not fit a conventional term-loan structure.

Balance Sheet Optimisation — Reassessing leverage, security utilisation and the mix of short-term and long-term borrowing.

Funding Consolidation — Bringing fragmented banking arrangements into a more coordinated financing structure.

Where Businesses Typically Need Corporate Finance Advisory

01

Funding Business Expansion

Growth requires capital before it generates additional cash flow. The financing challenge is to ensure that debt servicing does not begin placing pressure on the existing operating business before the expansion has reached a sustainable level. The structure may need to consider:

  • Capital expenditure cycle
  • Ramp-up period
  • Incremental EBITDA
  • Working capital requirements
  • Moratorium requirements
  • Repayment profile
02

Financing an Acquisition

Acquisition financing is not simply about borrowing against the target company's valuation. The structure needs to account for acquisition consideration, sponsor contribution, post-acquisition leverage, cash-flow integration and the legal relationship between the acquisition vehicle and operating entities. Depending on the transaction, considerations may include:

  • Acquisition debt
  • HoldCo financing
  • Operating-company debt
  • Senior or subordinated capital
  • Promoter contribution
  • Security creation
  • Post-acquisition refinancing
03

Refinancing Existing Debt

An existing facility may have been appropriate when it was originally sanctioned but may no longer suit the business. Common triggers include:

  • Short repayment tenure
  • High annual debt servicing
  • Excessive collateral utilisation
  • Restrictive covenants
  • Multiple uncoordinated facilities
  • Mismatch between borrowing tenor and asset life

Refinancing should not be viewed only as an interest-rate exercise. The objective may be to improve the overall financing architecture.

04

Supporting Promoter Equity Preservation

Debt can allow a business to fund growth without immediately diluting promoter ownership. However, avoiding equity dilution should not lead to excessive leverage. The relevant question is not whether debt is cheaper than equity in isolation — it is whether the business can comfortably service the proposed debt across different operating scenarios.

05

Managing Larger or Multi-Lender Requirements

When a financing requirement exceeds the exposure appetite of a single lender, the transaction may require participation from multiple institutions. This introduces additional structuring considerations:

  • Consortium versus multiple banking
  • Lead lender coordination
  • Common security
  • Pari-passu charge
  • Inter-creditor arrangements
  • Harmonisation of covenants
  • Documentation across lenders

The transaction becomes an execution exercise as much as a capital-raising exercise.

06

Restructuring a Changing Capital Requirement

Businesses evolve. Capital structures should evolve with them. A company may have accumulated facilities across different stages of growth — working capital limits, term loans, NBFC facilities and promoter-backed borrowings — without a single integrated view of leverage. A corporate finance review can help assess whether these facilities continue to serve the underlying business.

Why Corporate Capital Raising Becomes Difficult

The quality of a business does not automatically translate into a straightforward financing transaction. Several businesses encounter friction because the capital requirement and the credit narrative are not sufficiently aligned.

Debt Capacity Is Often Overestimated

A lender evaluates debt capacity based on repayment ability — not simply collateral availability or management's growth expectations. Future EBITDA may support the business case, but lenders will test how quickly those cash flows materialise and how resilient they remain under downside assumptions.

Growth Capital Can Create Cash-Flow Pressure

Expansion often requires upfront capital. The resulting cash flows may take time to stabilise. If the repayment profile begins too early, the existing business may end up subsidising debt service for a new investment that has not yet reached its intended operating capacity.

Existing Security May Already Be Fully Utilised

Companies frequently discover that their strongest assets are already charged to existing lenders. This creates a structuring challenge around residual collateral value, pari-passu arrangements, charge carve-outs, security sharing and release of excess collateral. Security should be evaluated as part of the overall capital structure — not as an afterthought.

Different Lenders May Have Different Risk Appetites

A transaction that does not fit one institution's underwriting framework is not necessarily unfinanceable. The issue may relate to sector appetite, ticket size, leverage tolerance, security requirements, tenor preference, acquisition financing policy or cash-flow profile. Identifying the right lender fit is therefore more valuable than indiscriminate lender outreach.

Financial Information May Not Tell the Full Credit Story

Historical financial statements are essential, but lenders also need to understand what is changing: why leverage is increasing, what the additional capital will achieve, how cash flows will change, what the downside scenario looks like and what protects the lender if performance underperforms. The transaction narrative has to connect historical performance with the proposed capital structure.

How Do Lenders Evaluate a Corporate Debt Transaction?

Corporate lenders assess more than the stated purpose of the borrowing. They evaluate whether the business can absorb additional leverage, continue servicing debt through operating cycles and maintain sufficient financial flexibility if performance does not develop exactly as projected. While underwriting varies by sector and institution, several considerations consistently influence corporate credit decisions.

Leverage — Debt / EBITDA

Debt relative to EBITDA is commonly used to understand the relationship between borrowing and operating earnings.

The number itself is not sufficient. Lenders also assess the quality of EBITDA, recurring versus non-recurring income, cyclicality, customer concentration and the sustainability of margins. A business with stable and predictable cash flows may support leverage differently from a business with volatile earnings.

Interest Coverage

Can operating earnings comfortably service interest obligations?

Interest Coverage Ratio (ICR) provides an indication of the company's ability to meet interest commitments from operating earnings. A weakening coverage ratio can become an early sign that leverage is beginning to constrain financial flexibility. Lenders may also evaluate the impact of higher interest rates, lower margins, delayed collections or reduced capacity utilisation.

Cash-Flow Conversion

Accounting profitability and cash generation are not the same. A company may report healthy EBITDA while a significant portion of cash remains tied up in receivables, inventory, capital expenditure, advances or group-company transactions. Credit underwriting therefore looks beyond reported profitability towards the cash available to meet financing obligations.

Total Outside Liabilities Relative to Net Worth

TOL/TNW is used to assess the relationship between a company's external liabilities and its underlying capital base. A higher ratio does not automatically make a transaction unacceptable, but it influences how lenders assess financial leverage and the capacity to absorb further obligations.

Promoter Contribution & Commitment

Where a transaction requires significant incremental capital, lenders generally evaluate how much risk the promoters are retaining. The assessment may extend beyond a simple equity contribution to include financial commitment, execution track record, governance, support history and alignment with the proposed transaction.

Security Structure

Security provides lender protection, but the structure matters. Credit teams may assess the quality of underlying assets, existing charges, priority of charge, ease of enforceability, adequacy of security cover and the availability of unencumbered assets. The objective is not always to pledge every available asset — a well-structured transaction should seek adequate lender protection without unnecessarily restricting the company's future financial flexibility.

Business & Management Quality

Corporate credit is not assessed only through ratios. Lenders also evaluate industry experience, management capability, governance practices, financial reporting discipline, customer concentration, related-party transactions and track record through previous business cycles.

Matching the Capital Structure to the Transaction

There is no single corporate financing product suitable for every requirement. The structure should reflect the purpose of capital, underlying cash flows and lender appetite.

How EquiFin Approaches a Corporate Finance Transaction

01

Understand the Transaction

We begin with the business objective. Is the capital intended for expansion, acquisition, refinancing, consolidation or another strategic requirement? Understanding the purpose of capital is essential before deciding the structure.

02

Assess Debt Capacity

The proposed transaction is evaluated against historical financial performance, existing leverage, operating cash flows, projected cash generation, debt servicing obligations and security availability, to understand what the business can realistically support.

03

Structure the Requirement

The financing structure may involve decisions around debt quantum, facility type, tenor, moratorium, amortisation, promoter contribution, security and covenants. A transaction should be structured before it is taken to the market.

04

Develop the Credit Narrative

Financial data alone does not explain a transaction. The lender needs to understand what is changing, why capital is required, how it will be deployed and how the resulting business will support the proposed debt.

05

Identify Lender Alignment

Different lenders have different sector preferences, risk parameters and transaction appetites. The objective is to approach relevant capital providers based on the characteristics of the transaction — not simply maximise the number of institutions contacted.

06

Support Credit Evaluation

During the appraisal process, lenders may require clarification on financial assumptions, cash-flow projections, existing debt, security, transaction structure and promoter contribution. This often involves refining how the transaction is presented while maintaining consistency with the underlying business reality.

07

Execute Through Financial Close

Sanction is an important milestone, but it is not the end of the transaction. Execution may continue through term sheet review, sanction condition evaluation, documentation, security creation, conditions precedent and disbursement coordination.

Important Financial Considerations

The metrics that influence corporate debt capacity:

EBITDA
Lenders evaluate the quality and sustainability of operating earnings — not just the reported number.
Debt / EBITDA
Helps assess the relationship between debt obligations and operating earnings. Interpretation varies by industry and cash-flow characteristics.
Interest Coverage Ratio
Measures the ability of operating earnings to cover interest obligations.
TOL/TNW
Provides a view of overall leverage relative to the company's capital base.
Free Cash Flow
Important for understanding how much cash remains after operating and investment requirements.
Debt Maturity Profile
A company may have acceptable leverage but still face refinancing risk if significant obligations mature within a short period.
Covenant Headroom
Financial covenants should be evaluated not only against current performance but also against realistic downside scenarios.
Security Utilisation
The value of collateral is important, but so is its future availability — over-encumbering the strongest assets can limit flexibility for future transactions.

Promoters and CFOs Often Look at the Same Transaction Differently

A Promoter May Ask

Can the business raise this capital without diluting ownership?

A CFO May Ask

Can the balance sheet and operating cash flows sustain it?

Both questions are valid. A well-structured corporate financing transaction should attempt to reconcile them. Too little leverage may constrain growth unnecessarily. Too much leverage may transfer the financing burden onto the operating business.

The objective is not to maximise debt. It is to determine an appropriate level of debt for the transaction and structure it around the economic reality of the business.

EquiFin Perspective

The cheapest debt is not always the most efficient capital. A marginally lower interest rate can become expensive if the repayment schedule is too aggressive, security requirements consume future borrowing capacity or restrictive covenants limit operational flexibility. Corporate finance should therefore be evaluated at the level of the complete capital structure — not only the quoted borrowing rate.

When Corporate Finance Advisory May Be Relevant

Consider a corporate finance review when:

  • The business is planning a significant expansion
  • An acquisition opportunity requires external capital
  • Existing debt has become expensive or restrictive
  • Repayment obligations are not aligned with cash generation
  • Multiple facilities have created a fragmented banking structure
  • Security availability is becoming a constraint

Frequently Asked Questions

What is corporate finance advisory?

Corporate finance advisory helps businesses evaluate and structure capital requirements related to growth, acquisitions, refinancing, balance-sheet optimisation and other strategic transactions. The work typically involves assessing debt capacity, structuring the financing requirement, preparing the transaction for institutional evaluation and supporting execution.

How is corporate debt capacity determined?

Debt capacity is generally assessed using a combination of operating cash flows, existing leverage, profitability, debt servicing obligations, financial ratios, security and the overall business risk profile. Lenders may use metrics such as Debt/EBITDA, interest coverage and leverage relative to net worth, while also evaluating the quality and sustainability of cash flows.

When should a company consider refinancing existing debt?

Refinancing may be relevant when the existing debt structure no longer aligns with the business. Common reasons include high annual repayments, short maturities, expensive legacy borrowing, excessive collateral utilisation or changes in the company's financial profile.

Can debt be used to finance business acquisitions?

Depending on the transaction structure and lender appetite, debt may form part of an acquisition financing structure. Lenders typically assess acquisition rationale, promoter contribution, post-transaction leverage, cash-flow generation, security and the ability of the combined business to service debt.

What is the difference between corporate finance and working capital finance?

Corporate finance generally addresses broader strategic capital requirements such as growth, acquisitions, refinancing and capital structure. Working capital finance focuses more specifically on funding the operating cycle, including inventory, receivables and trade-related requirements.

What do lenders look for before approving corporate debt?

Lenders typically evaluate financial performance, leverage, interest servicing ability, cash-flow stability, management quality, business risk, existing liabilities and the proposed security structure. The relative importance of each factor varies by transaction and sector.

Does collateral determine how much debt a company can raise?

Collateral can influence lender comfort and security cover, but it does not independently determine debt capacity. For most corporate lending transactions, lenders also assess whether the underlying business can generate sufficient cash flows to service the proposed borrowing.

Does EquiFin provide loans directly?

No. EquiFin acts as an independent corporate finance and debt advisory firm. Financing decisions, terms and approvals remain subject to the independent evaluation and credit processes of the relevant capital providers.
