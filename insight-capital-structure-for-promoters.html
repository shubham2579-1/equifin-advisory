How Should Promoters Think About Capital Structure?

Capital structure is often discussed as a choice between debt and equity. In reality, the more important question is whether the capital supporting a business is aligned with how that business grows, generates cash and absorbs risk.

10 Min Read·Perspective·September 2026

The Question

A growing business eventually reaches a point where its existing capital structure needs to be reconsidered. Revenue may be increasing, new capacity may be required, working capital may be expanding, an acquisition opportunity may emerge, or existing debt may be approaching maturity. At that stage, the conversation often begins with a familiar question: should we raise debt or equity? It is an important question. But it is rarely the best place to start.

A more useful question is: what kind of capital does the business need, what should that capital support, and how should it behave within the financial structure of the company? Capital structure is not simply about finding the cheapest source of money. It is about creating an appropriate relationship between:

  • Business risk
  • Cash flows
  • Asset life
  • Growth requirements
  • Financial obligations
  • Ownership considerations
  • Future flexibility

For promoters, this distinction matters because capital decisions made today can influence the company’s strategic options for years.

Why Capital Structure Deserves More Attention

Businesses often make financing decisions transaction by transaction. A working capital facility is arranged when liquidity tightens. A term loan is raised when capacity needs to be expanded. Additional debt is considered when growth accelerates. Equity may be explored when leverage begins to feel uncomfortable. Individually, each decision may appear rational. But capital structure problems often emerge when these decisions are made without considering the cumulative effect. Over time, a company may find itself with:

  • Short-term debt funding long-term requirements
  • Significant repayment obligations concentrated within a narrow period
  • Working capital facilities supporting structural cash deficits
  • Valuable assets with limited financial flexibility
  • Strong EBITDA but insufficient free cash flow
  • Growth plans constrained by existing leverage
  • Multiple lenders with overlapping security interests

The issue is not necessarily that the company has too much debt. Sometimes, the problem is that the wrong type of capital is supporting the wrong requirement. That is why capital structure should be viewed as an ongoing strategic framework—not simply a financing decision made when funds are required.

Capital Structure Is About Matching Capital With Purpose

At its simplest level, different business requirements have different financial characteristics. A short-term operating requirement does not necessarily need the same type of capital as a long-life infrastructure asset. Similarly, an acquisition may require a different financing approach from recurring inventory requirements. The objective is not to apply a standard financing product to every situation — it is to understand whether the nature of capital matches the economic purpose it is funding.

01 — What Is the Capital Actually Funding?

The first question sounds obvious, but it is often underestimated. A company may say, “We require ₹100 crore for expansion.” But expansion can mean many different things. It could include:

  • Land acquisition
  • New manufacturing capacity
  • Equipment purchase
  • Additional inventory
  • Technology investment
  • Distribution infrastructure
  • Working capital
  • Acquisition of another business

Each component may have a different risk profile and cash-flow cycle. A financing requirement becomes easier to structure when the underlying purpose of capital is understood with precision. The question is not simply how much capital is needed — it is what exactly this capital is expected to create.

02 — When Will the Investment Begin Generating Cash?

The timing of cash generation is central to capital structure. Suppose a company invests today but the underlying asset will begin generating meaningful cash flows only after several years. Immediate and aggressive repayment obligations could create pressure before the investment has had sufficient time to mature. On the other hand, a business requirement that converts into cash quickly may not require long-tenor capital. This creates a fundamental principle: the timing of capital repayment should be considered alongside the timing of cash generation. A financing structure should therefore examine when capital is deployed, when the asset becomes operational, when cash generation begins, how predictable it is, and when repayment obligations commence. This is particularly important in infrastructure, real estate, manufacturing expansion, renewable energy, hospitality and large-scale project development.

03 — How Much Financial Risk Can the Business Comfortably Carry?

Debt can accelerate growth. But it also introduces fixed financial obligations. The important question is not whether a company can raise more debt — it is how much financial obligation the business can absorb across different operating scenarios. A business may perform comfortably under its base-case projections, but capital structure should also consider what happens when revenue growth slows, margins decline, collections are delayed, project implementation is extended, interest costs increase, or capex exceeds expectations. Financial leverage introduces sensitivity: the higher the debt burden, the greater the importance of understanding downside scenarios. This does not mean businesses should avoid debt. It means debt should be evaluated as part of a broader risk framework.

04 — How Much Flexibility Will the Business Need Tomorrow?

Capital decisions should not be evaluated only against today’s requirement. A promoter should also consider what happens after this transaction — will the business require additional capex, is an acquisition being considered, will working capital requirements increase, are significant debt maturities approaching, will the company need to provide additional security, and could future financing become more difficult? A capital structure that solves today’s requirement but restricts tomorrow’s options may not be strategically efficient. Financial flexibility has value, and it may come from:

  • Unencumbered assets
  • Manageable leverage
  • Diversified funding relationships
  • Longer maturity profiles
  • Predictable cash flows
  • Covenant headroom
  • Ability to raise incremental capital

This flexibility can become particularly valuable when unexpected opportunities or challenges emerge.

Debt Is Not Just Capital. It Is a Commitment.

Businesses often evaluate debt primarily through its cost. Interest rate comparisons are important. But the economic cost of debt extends beyond the coupon or lending rate. Debt also creates commitments around:

  • Interest servicing
  • Principal repayment
  • Financial covenants
  • Security
  • Information requirements
  • Restrictions on certain transactions
  • Refinancing timelines

Therefore, a lower-cost financing facility is not automatically a better financing solution. A structure with a lower interest rate but inappropriate tenor, excessive amortisation, restrictive covenants or significant collateral requirements may create challenges that are not immediately visible at the point of borrowing. The true question is whether the structure works for the business — not only today, but through the life of the financing.

Equity Is Not Free Capital

The opposite mistake is to view equity as “risk-free” simply because it does not create mandatory repayment obligations. Equity has its own economic and strategic considerations. Raising equity can involve:

  • Ownership dilution
  • Governance considerations
  • Decision-making rights
  • Return expectations
  • Future exit expectations

Therefore, debt versus equity should not be framed simply as “debt is expensive, equity is free.” Neither statement is accurate. Both forms of capital have costs. The difference is often in how and when those costs are realised. Debt creates contractual financial obligations. Equity can involve long-term ownership and value-sharing implications. The appropriate decision depends on the specific circumstances of the business and transaction.

The Promoter Perspective: Control vs Financial Flexibility

For promoter-led businesses, capital structure often has an additional dimension: ownership matters. Promoters may prefer debt because it allows the business to raise capital without immediate equity dilution — a rational consideration. However, preserving ownership at all costs can sometimes create excessive financial leverage. Similarly, avoiding debt entirely to maintain financial conservatism may result in unnecessary equity dilution or slower growth. The objective should not be maximum promoter ownership, or minimum debt. The objective should be a capital structure that supports growth without creating an unsustainable financial burden or unnecessarily compromising strategic flexibility. That balance is different for every company.

Capital Structure Is Also a Timing Decision

Two businesses may have similar financial profiles but very different financing outcomes because of timing. Consider the difference between a company that begins preparing for refinancing eighteen months before a major maturity, and a company that begins approaching lenders three months before repayment is due. The underlying business may be identical, but the negotiating environment is not. Capital providers generally need time to understand the business, review financial information, evaluate projections, complete due diligence, conduct internal approvals and finalise documentation. Urgency can change the dynamics of a transaction. For this reason, capital planning should ideally begin before capital becomes immediately necessary — the strongest financing decisions are often made when the business still has options.

A Useful Framework: Think in Layers

Layer 01 — Operating Capital

Capital required to support the normal operating cycle — receivables, inventory, procurement, operating expenses. Key consideration: does the financing structure reflect the business’s cash conversion cycle?

Layer 02 — Growth Capital

Capital required to expand the business — new facilities, capacity expansion, market entry, technology investment. Key consideration: when will the investment begin contributing to cash generation?

Layer 03 — Strategic Capital

Capital deployed for significant corporate actions — acquisitions, business restructuring, major expansion, new strategic initiatives. Key consideration: what is the long-term strategic and financial impact of the transaction?

Layer 04 — Financial Resilience

Capital capacity available to absorb unexpected events — liquidity buffers, undrawn facilities, unencumbered assets, refinancing capacity. Key consideration: how much flexibility does the company retain after the current transaction?

The Risk of Looking at Leverage in Isolation

Financial ratios are useful, but no single ratio can fully explain capital structure risk. Debt-to-EBITDA can provide a useful indication of leverage, but it does not independently explain working capital intensity, future capex, debt maturity concentration, cash-flow volatility, asset quality or industry cyclicality. Similarly, interest coverage can provide insight into debt servicing capacity but may not capture future principal repayments. The purpose of financial analysis is not simply to achieve a particular ratio — it is to understand the economic reality behind the numbers. A business with higher leverage but stable, predictable cash flows may have a different risk profile from a business with lower leverage but highly volatile cash generation. Context matters.

Growth Can Quietly Change the Capital Structure

One of the most important situations for promoters to monitor is rapid growth. Growth is generally positive, but growth can consume capital. A company expanding revenue may require higher inventory, longer receivables, additional employees, new facilities, equipment and distribution capacity. If growth consumes cash faster than internal generation can support it, the business may become increasingly dependent on external financing. The challenge is that financial stress can emerge during periods of strong operating performance — a business may report higher revenue, higher EBITDA and increased market share while simultaneously experiencing higher receivables, increased inventory, greater short-term borrowing and pressure on operating cash flows. This is why growth strategy and capital strategy should be considered together.

Capital Structure Should Evolve With the Business

There is no permanent “correct” capital structure. A structure that works for a growing company with ₹100 crore of revenue may not be appropriate when the business reaches ₹1,000 crore. As companies evolve, scale, cash-flow predictability, asset base, institutional access, governance requirements and growth ambitions all change — and capital strategy should evolve accordingly. A business should periodically reassess its existing debt, maturity profile, funding concentration, security utilisation, financial flexibility and future capital requirements. Capital structure is therefore not a one-time exercise. It is part of long-term financial strategy.

EquiFin Perspective

The best capital structure is not necessarily the one with the lowest cost of capital or the least dilution. It is the one that allows the business to pursue its strategy while keeping financial obligations aligned with its ability to generate cash and absorb risk.

Key Takeaways

  • Capital structure is more than a debt-versus-equity decision.
  • The type and tenor of capital should be considered alongside the purpose it is funding and the timing of expected cash flows.
  • Debt should be evaluated not only by interest cost but also by its broader financial commitments.
  • Growth can increase capital requirements and create liquidity pressure even when profitability improves.
  • Financial flexibility—including maturity management, security availability and liquidity—has strategic value.
  • Capital planning is generally more effective when undertaken before funding requirements become urgent.
  • There is no universally correct capital structure. The appropriate structure depends on the business, sector, cash-flow characteristics and strategic objectives.

Closing Perspective

Capital is often discussed at the point when it is needed. But by then, many of the important strategic choices may already have been made. Promoters and CFOs benefit from thinking about capital structure earlier—as part of the broader business strategy.

The question is not simply how much capital the company can raise. A more useful question is: what capital structure will allow the company to grow, absorb uncertainty and retain the flexibility required for its next stage? That is where capital strategy becomes more than financing. It becomes part of how a business is built.
