Real Estate Funding Begins Before Construction Finance

For many developers, the first significant capital requirement is not construction. It begins with securing the development opportunity itself.

A land parcel may need to be acquired. Development rights may need to be secured. Premiums and statutory payments may become payable before meaningful project revenue exists. Transferable Development Rights may need to be acquired to unlock the project's development potential. By the time construction begins, a significant amount of promoter capital may already be committed.

This creates an important financing challenge: how should capital be structured across the period before the project begins generating customer collections? The answer cannot be a standard construction loan. Different stages of the development lifecycle carry different forms of risk.

From Land to Monetisation: How Real Estate Capital Changes

Real estate development does not have one funding requirement. It has a sequence of capital requirements.

Stage 1

Land Acquisition

The developer identifies the underlying development opportunity. Capital may be required for outright land acquisition, acquisition of development rights, structured land transactions, consolidation of parcels and acquisition-related obligations. At this stage, the asset may not yet have project approvals, construction progress, customer bookings or project cash flows. The financing proposition is therefore fundamentally different from an operating or construction-stage asset.

Stage 2

Development Rights & Project Preparation

In some transactions, the developer's economic interest is based on development rights rather than outright land ownership. Capital requirements may arise around development agreements, joint development structures, rights acquisition, upfront consideration and project preparation. The financing structure needs to reflect the legal and commercial nature of the developer's interest.

Stage 3

Approvals

Before construction can begin, a project may require multiple regulatory and development approvals. The period between land acquisition and project readiness can create a significant capital lock-up. The developer may have already committed substantial capital while construction cannot yet commence, sales may not yet begin and customer collections do not exist. This approval-stage funding gap is often structurally different from conventional project finance.

Stage 4

Premium Payments

Development projects can involve significant premium and statutory payment obligations. These obligations can create concentrated capital requirements before the project begins generating operating or customer cash flows. The financing challenge is therefore not simply the availability of security — it is the timing mismatch between a substantial capital outflow today and project monetisation that may occur significantly later. Premium funding needs to be evaluated within the broader development capital structure.

Stage 5

TDR Acquisition

Transferable Development Rights can represent a strategic component of the project's development potential. Where applicable, acquiring TDR may require substantial upfront capital. The financing consideration involves understanding the project's development potential, the role of TDR within the project, timing of utilisation, existing project capital commitments and expected monetisation of the resulting development potential. TDR should not be treated as an isolated transaction — it should be evaluated as part of the overall capital required to unlock and develop the underlying project.

Stage 6

Construction

Once the project moves into active development, the capital requirement becomes more closely linked to construction progress, project cost, contractor obligations, sales velocity and collection efficiency. This is where conventional construction finance may become relevant.

Stage 7

Completion & Monetisation

As construction risk reduces, the financing conversation can shift towards last-mile capital, inventory funding, refinancing and asset monetisation. For stabilised commercial assets, long-term financing may eventually be structured around contracted rental cash flows.

Where Developers Typically Need Capital

Real estate transactions where funding structure matters:

Signature Insight 01

Land Acquisition Funding

Land acquisition is often the starting point of a real estate development strategy. However, it is also one of the most difficult stages to finance conventionally because the asset may not yet have development approvals, construction progress, project-level cash flows or customer bookings. The capital structure may therefore need to consider transaction structure, land value, legal title, location, development potential, promoter contribution and exit or monetisation visibility.

The core financing question: can the proposed capital structure support the acquisition period until the asset progresses into its next value-creation stage? This is significantly different from asking whether a completed property has sufficient collateral value.

02

Development Rights Funding

Not every real estate transaction begins with outright land acquisition. A developer may obtain the right to develop a property through commercial or contractual arrangements. Capital requirements may arise from development agreements, upfront consideration, rights acquisition, joint development structures and redevelopment arrangements. The financing structure needs to reflect the nature of the developer's economic interest and the pathway towards project monetisation.

Signature Insight 03

Approvals Funding

The period between land acquisition and construction readiness can consume significant promoter capital. Capital may remain locked while the developer progresses through planning, regulatory processes, project preparation, documentation and technical work. The challenge is particularly important because this period may not yet generate meaningful project cash inflows.

The funding gap: capital is deployed, but the project has not yet reached the stage where conventional construction finance or customer collections become available. Approval-stage funding requires a different assessment of development risk.

Signature Insight 04

Premium Funding

Premium and statutory obligations can create large, concentrated cash requirements. For a developer, the challenge may not be whether the payment is commercially justified — it may be whether making the payment significantly reduces liquidity available for land acquisition, project mobilisation, construction, working capital and other development opportunities. Premium funding can therefore become a capital allocation decision.

The structuring question: should promoter equity be permanently locked into a statutory payment, or can the broader capital stack be structured more efficiently? Any financing structure remains dependent on transaction specifics and independent capital-provider evaluation.

Signature Insight 05

TDR Funding

TDR acquisition can require capital deployment before the economic benefits of additional development potential are realised. The funding assessment may need to consider project economics, TDR requirement, timing of utilisation, development potential created, project approvals, overall leverage and expected project monetisation. TDR funding is not merely an asset purchase — it can be part of the capital required to unlock the project's future development potential.

06

Construction Finance

Once development risk reduces and the project enters active construction, financing can be structured around total project cost, construction milestones, promoter contribution, sales collections, drawdown requirements and remaining construction cost. The objective is to align capital deployment with physical project execution.

07

Last-Mile Funding

Projects approaching completion can face liquidity pressure despite substantial value already being created. Funding may be required to bridge remaining construction cost, delayed collections and final execution obligations. At this stage, the lender's assessment may focus heavily on physical completion, remaining cost, unsold inventory, expected collections and time to completion.

08

Inventory Funding

Completed inventory can represent capital that remains locked until monetisation. Financing may be evaluated against available inventory, market absorption, expected sales realisations, existing debt and security cover. The relevant question is not simply the notional value of inventory — it is how reliably that inventory can convert into cash during the proposed financing period.

09

Refinancing

A project's financing requirements can change as risk reduces. Early-stage capital may eventually need to be replaced once the project achieves approvals, construction progress, sales traction and collection visibility. Refinancing may help realign the capital structure with the project's current — not historical — risk profile.

10

Lease Rental Discounting

Stabilised commercial assets with contracted lease rentals represent a different financing proposition. The focus shifts from development and construction risk towards tenant quality, lease tenure, occupancy, rental cash flows and property value. LRD is covered separately under EquiFin's Lease Rental Discounting service.

The Most Difficult Capital Requirement Often Comes Before Construction

A completed asset has value. An under-construction project has physical progress. A stabilised project may have sales collections or rental cash flows. But an early-stage development opportunity may have none of these.

The developer may still need to commit capital towards:

  • Land
  • Development rights
  • Approvals
  • Premiums
  • TDR
  • Project preparation

This creates what can be described as an early-stage development capital gap. At this point, asset monetisation is still in the future, customer collections may not exist, construction finance may not yet be available and promoter capital is heavily utilised.

The challenge is to structure capital across this period without creating excessive pressure on the project's future economics. This is where real estate funding advisory requires a different perspective from conventional construction lending.

How Do Capital Providers Evaluate Early-Stage Real Estate Transactions?

Early-stage real estate financing cannot be evaluated solely on current cash flows because those cash flows may not yet exist. Capital providers therefore focus more heavily on the underlying development proposition and the developer's ability to move the asset through subsequent stages.

1. Legal Title

For land-related transactions, the legal position is fundamental. Evaluation may involve title documentation, search reports, existing encumbrances, development rights, ownership structure and existing lender charges. The quality of legal documentation can materially influence both lender appetite and transaction timelines.

2. Development Potential

The value proposition may depend on what can ultimately be developed. Capital providers may seek clarity around permissible development, project configuration, development potential, applicable regulations and proposed asset type. The underlying question is: what is the pathway from the current asset to future monetisation?

3. Approval Visibility

For early-stage projects, approvals can be a significant source of uncertainty. Lenders and capital providers may evaluate current approval status, pending approvals, dependencies, historical timelines and execution capability. Approval risk directly affects the timing of future construction and monetisation.

4. Promoter Contribution

When a project is still pre-revenue, promoter commitment becomes particularly important. Capital providers may assess capital already deployed, remaining equity commitment, land contribution, ability to fund contingencies and track record. The objective is to understand the degree of alignment between the promoter and external capital.

5. Development Timeline

Time is a critical variable in early-stage real estate financing. A delay can affect carrying costs, interest costs, project launch, customer collections and refinancing options. The development timeline therefore needs to be evaluated realistically.

6. Exit or Take-Out Visibility

Early-stage capital often requires a credible transition into the next stage of financing. Capital providers may therefore consider construction finance potential, project launch visibility, sales potential, refinancing possibilities and asset monetisation strategy. The transaction is evaluated not only on today's position, but on how the asset is expected to progress.

7. Security & Capital Protection

Depending on the transaction, capital providers may examine asset security, security cover, existing charges, priority of security and cash-flow controls. Collateral integrity is one of the core institutional underwriting principles for early-stage real estate transactions.

Signature Insight

Approvals, Premiums & TDR: Development Capital Requirements

One common mistake in capital planning is to treat approvals, premiums and TDR as secondary project expenses. From a financing perspective, they can represent major capital events. They may occur before project revenues begin, require substantial upfront payments, lock promoter capital, affect the project's overall leverage and influence the timing of construction commencement. They therefore need to be incorporated into the capital structure from the beginning.

Approval Capital

Approval-stage expenditure needs to be considered within overall project cost, development timeline, promoter liquidity and expected funding transitions.

Premium Capital

Premium obligations can create concentrated liquidity requirements. The structure should consider their impact on sponsor equity availability, project leverage, future construction funding and overall project returns.

TDR Capital

TDR acquisition should be considered against development potential created, timing of utilisation, project economics and future monetisation.

A project can be financially undercapitalised before construction even begins. If land acquisition, development rights, approvals, premiums and TDR consume disproportionate promoter capital, the project may enter construction with insufficient financial flexibility. Strong real estate capital planning begins before the first construction milestone.

How EquiFin Approaches a Real Estate Funding Transaction

01

Understand the Development Stage

We begin by identifying where the asset sits in its development lifecycle — has land been acquired, are development rights secured, what approvals are pending, are premium obligations due, is TDR required, has construction commenced and what capital has already been deployed. The financing structure should reflect the actual stage of the development, not a generic real estate loan category.

02

Map the Full Capital Requirement

The visible funding requirement is not always the complete funding requirement. A developer may approach for land acquisition capital while significant additional requirements remain for approvals, premiums, TDR, project mobilisation and construction. We look at the broader development capital requirement.

03

Assess Capital Provider Readiness

Different stages attract different types of capital appetite. The transaction assessment may consider development stage, legal clarity, approval visibility, promoter contribution, security and future project economics, identifying the appropriate financing conversation before extensive market outreach.

04

Structure the Transaction

Key structuring considerations may include funding quantum, purpose of capital, promoter contribution, tenor, repayment, security and future refinancing pathway. The structure should consider not only the current funding event but the project's subsequent capital requirements.

05

Develop the Credit Narrative

A lender or capital provider needs to understand what is being acquired or funded, why capital is required now, what development potential exists, what approvals are relevant, how the project will progress and what the future monetisation pathway is.

06

Align Appropriate Capital Providers

Capital-provider appetite can vary significantly depending on whether the requirement relates to land, development rights, approvals, premiums, TDR, construction or stabilised assets. The objective is targeted lender alignment, not indiscriminate outreach.

07

Execute Through Disbursement

Transaction execution may involve due diligence, legal documentation, security creation, conditions precedent, existing lender coordination and drawdown requirements — continuing until committed capital can actually be deployed for the intended transaction.

Key Underwriting & Financial Considerations

The metrics and considerations behind real estate funding:

Loan-to-Cost

Loan-to-Cost compares proposed financing with the overall cost of the project or transaction, providing a perspective on leverage, sponsor commitment and capital cushion. Illustrative real estate LTC ranges are often cited around 55–60%, though actual thresholds vary materially by transaction and capital provider.

Loan-to-Value

Loan-to-Value compares financing exposure with the assessed value of the underlying security. For early-stage transactions, however, headline asset value alone may not determine financing capacity — legal clarity and monetisation visibility matter equally.

Promoter Contribution

Promoter capital provides alignment and financial cushioning. Capital providers may assess amount already invested, remaining contribution, source of funds and ability to support contingencies.

Approval Risk

The value of an early-stage development can depend heavily on approvals. Delays can affect capital carrying cost, project launch, construction commencement and future refinancing.

Development Timeline

A realistic timeline is critical. The longer capital remains deployed before monetisation, the greater the pressure on the project's carrying cost.

Sales-to-Construction Progress

For active projects, lenders may compare sales and collections against construction progress — a relevant real estate underwriting consideration alongside escrow cash-flow cover.

Escrow Cash-Flow Visibility

For applicable project financing structures, lenders may examine how project collections flow and whether they provide adequate visibility for financing obligations.

From Land Capital to Construction Capital: The Critical Transition

One of the most important real estate financing decisions happens before construction finance is required. A developer may initially raise or deploy capital for land, development rights, premiums, TDR and approvals. But eventually, the project needs to transition into construction. That transition needs planning.

The question is: will the early-stage capital structure allow the project to attract construction-stage capital later? An inefficient early-stage capital structure can create problems such as:

  • Excessive leverage before construction
  • High carrying costs
  • Restrictive security obligations
  • Insufficient promoter liquidity
  • Difficulty in refinancing

The development capital stack therefore needs to be viewed as a sequence:

A

Secure the development opportunity.

B

Fund approvals, premiums and development requirements.

C

Transition into construction capital.

D

Move towards sales and collection-led deleveraging.

E

Refinance or monetise mature assets where appropriate.

The objective is not simply to arrange capital at each stage. It is to avoid creating a capital structure at one stage that prevents financing flexibility at the next.

EquiFin Perspective

Real estate funding should be planned as a capital sequence, not a series of isolated loans. The financing decision made at land acquisition can affect promoter liquidity during approvals. Approval-stage capital can influence construction leverage. Construction debt can determine how much flexibility remains during project completion. The strongest capital structures preserve financing options as the asset progresses.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is land acquisition funding?

Land acquisition funding refers to capital structured for acquiring land or securing a real estate development opportunity. The appropriate structure depends on factors such as legal title, transaction structure, development potential, promoter contribution and the expected pathway towards project development or monetisation.

Can financing be structured for development rights?

Depending on the legal and commercial structure of the transaction, capital may be required to secure development rights, development agreements or other rights associated with a real estate project. Financing feasibility depends on transaction-specific underwriting and capital-provider evaluation.

What is approval-stage funding in real estate?

Approval-stage funding addresses capital requirements that arise between acquiring or securing a development opportunity and reaching construction readiness. During this period, promoter capital may be deployed while the project has not yet begun generating construction-stage or customer cash flows.

What is premium funding?

Premium funding refers to capital structured around significant premium or statutory payment obligations associated with a development transaction. The financing structure needs to consider the timing of the payment, the broader project capital requirement and future project monetisation.

What is TDR funding?

TDR funding refers to capital requirements associated with acquiring Transferable Development Rights where applicable to a real estate development. The transaction should generally be evaluated in the context of the project's development potential, overall economics and capital structure.

Why is early-stage real estate funding more difficult?

At the land and approval stage, a project may not yet have construction progress, customer bookings or operating cash flows. Capital providers therefore rely more heavily on legal clarity, development potential, approval visibility, promoter commitment and the future pathway towards project monetisation.

How does construction finance differ from land funding?

Construction finance is generally evaluated against project cost, construction progress, sales and collections. Land and early-stage funding may be evaluated more heavily on the underlying asset, legal structure, development potential, approvals and the sponsor's ability to progress the project into its next stage.

Can early-stage real estate debt be refinanced later?

A project's risk profile may change significantly after approvals, construction progress and sales visibility are established. Refinancing may therefore become relevant as the project transitions from one development stage to another, subject to the transaction structure and independent lender evaluation.

Does EquiFin provide real estate loans directly?

No. EquiFin acts as an independent advisory firm supporting capital structuring and transaction execution. Financing decisions, terms and approvals remain subject to the independent processes of relevant lenders and capital providers.
