Why Financing Structure Matters as Much as the Amount of Capital Raised

Securing capital can solve an immediate funding requirement. But the long-term success of a financing transaction often depends on something equally important: whether the structure of that capital works with the business, its cash flows and the purpose it is intended to support.

10 Min Read·Perspective·September 2026

The Question

When businesses approach the market for financing, the conversation often begins with a number — “we need ₹100 crore.” The immediate focus then becomes which institution can provide it, at what interest rate, and how quickly it can be sanctioned. These questions are important, but raising the required amount of capital does not automatically mean the financing decision is optimal.

Consider two companies that both raise ₹100 crore. The first receives financing with a seven-year tenor, repayment aligned with expected cash generation, appropriate moratorium during implementation, clearly defined security and reasonable financial flexibility. The second receives the same amount with a shorter maturity, immediate repayment obligations, significant security encumbrance, restrictive covenants and limited room for future borrowing. Both companies have raised ₹100 crore, but they have not entered into the same financial transaction. The amount is identical; the financial consequences may be very different. That is why financing should not be evaluated only by how much capital is raised — it should also be evaluated by how that capital behaves after it enters the business.

Why Structure Matters

Capital does not exist independently from its terms. Every financing arrangement comes with a set of economic and contractual characteristics, which may include tenor, interest cost, repayment schedule, moratorium, security, covenants, prepayment conditions, drawdown flexibility, guarantees and conditions precedent. Together, these elements determine the practical behaviour of the capital. A financing structure that appears attractive at the time of sanction can create pressure later if it does not align with the underlying economics of the business — a long-life asset funded with short-tenor debt may create refinancing pressure, a project with a delayed cash-generation cycle may struggle under immediate amortisation, a growing business may find its future borrowing capacity constrained because too many assets have already been pledged, and a lower interest rate may appear attractive but be accompanied by conditions that reduce operational flexibility. The objective is therefore not simply to raise capital — it is to create a financing structure that can coexist with the operating and financial realities of the business.

The Amount of Capital Is Only One Variable

A strong financing decision considers the interaction between amount, tenor, repayment, moratorium, security, covenants and flexibility. Optimising one variable at the expense of all others can create unintended consequences — the lowest interest rate may come with restrictive conditions, the longest tenor may involve higher overall financing costs, the maximum loan amount may increase financial obligations beyond a comfortable level, and minimal equity contribution may increase leverage and reduce resilience. The objective is rarely to maximise a single term. It is to create an appropriate balance across the structure.

The Cost of Capital Is More Than the Interest Rate

Interest rate is naturally an important consideration, but evaluating financing purely on headline pricing can create an incomplete picture. A facility with a lower headline rate may still create a less favourable overall structure if it imposes significant operational or financial constraints.

01 — Tenor Should Reflect the Economic Life of the Requirement

One of the most important structural considerations is financing tenor. The appropriate tenor depends on the underlying use of capital — a short-term requirement may not need long-term financing, while a long-term asset may create pressure if financed through a short maturity. A working capital requirement is linked to the movement of the business and its cash conversion cycle, whereas a manufacturing expansion involves capital deployed today, a construction and installation period, and cash flows that build only gradually once commercial production begins. Applying the same financing approach to both requirements may not be appropriate. This leads to an important principle: the maturity of capital should be considered in relation to the life and cash-generation profile of what it is funding.

02 — Repayment Profile Can Matter More Than the Headline Amount

Businesses often focus heavily on the total debt raised, but financial pressure is created through periodic obligations. A ₹100 crore loan is not economically identical under every repayment schedule — gradual repayment over several years creates a very different annual cash obligation than aggressive amortisation beginning immediately, even though the headline borrowing amount is the same. This is particularly important for businesses with seasonal revenues, long project cycles, uneven cash flows or significant growth investments. The key question is not simply how much debt is being raised — it is what the business will need to pay, and when.

03 — Moratorium Can Be Important When Cash Flows Take Time

Some investments require capital before they generate returns — project construction, manufacturing capacity expansion, real estate development, infrastructure development and new hospitality assets. In such situations, the business deploys capital during an implementation period, but the asset may not immediately generate sufficient cash. A financing structure that requires significant principal repayment before cash flows begin can create a mismatch, which is where an appropriate moratorium or repayment commencement period becomes relevant. The objective is not simply to defer obligations — it is to understand whether the repayment timeline reflects the actual development and cash-generation cycle.

04 — Security Has a Strategic Cost

Security is often discussed primarily as a lender requirement. From the borrower’s perspective, however, security also has strategic implications — once an asset is pledged, its availability for future transactions may be affected. This becomes particularly relevant for companies that expect to require additional capital. Businesses need to consider which assets are being charged, whether assets are already encumbered, what unencumbered value remains, whether future lenders will require additional security, and whether the security package is proportionate to the transaction. The objective is not necessarily to avoid providing security — it is to understand the long-term implications of security utilisation. Collateral is not only a credit consideration; it is also part of a company’s future financial flexibility.

05 — Covenants Can Shape Future Decision-Making

Financial covenants are often treated as technical provisions within financing documentation, but they can have practical implications. Depending on the transaction, covenants may relate to leverage, debt servicing, additional borrowing, asset disposals, related-party transactions, dividend distributions or changes in business structure. Their presence is not inherently negative — they are part of many institutional financing arrangements. However, management should understand how these conditions interact with future business plans — a company may plan an acquisition, but existing documentation may restrict additional debt without lender consent, or management may expect to sell an asset that is part of the security package. The question is not whether covenants exist. It is whether management understands how the financing structure may affect future strategic decisions.

06 — Flexibility Has Financial Value

Businesses often focus on measurable financing terms — interest rate, loan amount, tenor. But flexibility can also have value, including the ability to prepay, additional drawdown capacity, scope for future borrowing, limited restrictions on asset utilisation, reasonable covenant headroom and the ability to restructure under changing circumstances. These benefits may not always appear in the initial comparison of financing offers, but they can become important over time. A financing arrangement should therefore be evaluated not only under the expected scenario — management should also consider what happens if growth is faster, if a new acquisition opportunity emerges, if cash generation is delayed, or if additional capex becomes necessary. A rigid structure can become a constraint even when the original financing decision appeared attractive.

Financing Structure Should Reflect Cash-Flow Reality

The most important relationship in transaction structuring is often the relationship between cash inflows and financial obligations — capital deployment, an implementation period, operations commencing, cash flows building, debt servicing capacity and, eventually, principal repayment. The timing will differ across businesses, but debt obligations should be considered against the expected pattern of cash generation. This becomes particularly important when businesses rely on optimistic projections: a financing structure may work perfectly in the base case, but capital planning should also consider reasonable variations — what happens if revenue is delayed, construction takes longer, margins decline, receivables increase, or interest costs rise? The answer can materially influence whether a proposed structure is appropriate.

Why “Maximum Funding” Is Not Always the Objective

Businesses sometimes approach financing with a goal of raising the maximum amount available. This can be understandable — additional liquidity provides comfort, and growth opportunities may require capital. However, more debt also creates more financial obligations. The relevant question is not how much debt the business can obtain — it may be how much debt the business requires, and what level it can sustainably support. Maximum borrowing capacity and optimal borrowing capacity are not always the same. An appropriate financing structure should consider the actual capital requirement, internal cash generation, future investment needs, existing leverage and downside resilience. The objective should be strategic sufficiency—not simply maximum leverage.

Structure Can Influence the Lender’s View as Well

Transaction structuring is not relevant only to the borrower — it can also influence institutional comfort. A lender may be more comfortable when the use of funds is clearly defined, repayment aligns with cash flows, equity contribution is appropriate, security is clearly identified and financial assumptions are explainable. A poorly structured proposal may create additional concerns even when the underlying business is strong. This is why transaction structuring involves understanding both perspectives — the business question of what capital the company needs to achieve its objectives, and the institutional question of what structure provides an acceptable relationship between risk and repayment. The transaction often sits between these two perspectives.

The Role of Capital Structure in Future Optionality

Every financing transaction affects the starting point for the next one. After raising capital, a company may have higher leverage, new repayment obligations, additional security encumbrances, financial covenants and new lender relationships — all of which influence future financial decisions. Promoters and CFOs should therefore consider the financing transaction within a broader capital roadmap, asking what additional funding may be required over the next three to five years, when existing debt will mature, whether future projects will require separate capital, how much security remains available, and whether additional leverage could be required. The objective is to avoid viewing each financing transaction as an isolated event. Capital decisions accumulate.

EquiFin Perspective

The amount of capital raised solves a funding requirement. The structure of that capital determines how the financing will interact with the business over time.

Key Takeaways

  • The amount of capital raised is only one component of a financing transaction.
  • Tenor and repayment schedules should be evaluated against the economic life and cash-generation profile of the underlying requirement.
  • A lower interest rate does not automatically mean a better financing structure.
  • Security utilisation can affect future borrowing flexibility.
  • Financial covenants should be understood in the context of future business plans.
  • Moratorium and repayment timing can be important where investments require time before generating cash flows.
  • Maximum borrowing capacity is not necessarily the same as optimal borrowing capacity.
  • A financing structure should be evaluated not only against the base case but also against reasonable downside scenarios.

Closing Perspective

Raising capital is an important milestone. But the transaction does not end when funds are disbursed — that is when the structure begins to matter. Repayment schedules begin. Covenants become active. Assets become encumbered. Financial flexibility changes.

The real test of a financing structure is therefore not whether the capital was successfully raised. It is whether the business can continue to operate, grow and make strategic decisions without the structure itself becoming a constraint. For promoters and CFOs, the question should not end with how much capital can be raised — it should continue with what structure will allow this capital to support the business without compromising its future financial flexibility. That is where financing becomes transaction structuring.
