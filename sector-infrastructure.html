Infrastructure Is Built With Capital Before It Is Supported by Cash Flow

Infrastructure financing has a fundamental timing challenge. The majority of capital is deployed upfront. Cash flows may begin only after construction, commissioning, regulatory approvals and operationalisation. This creates a long gap between capital deployment and cash-flow generation.

Project Award → Development → Financial Close → Construction → Commissioning → Operational Ramp-Up → Long-Term Cash Flows

The financing strategy must survive every stage. The objective is not simply to arrange debt for an infrastructure project. It is to ensure that the project's capital structure remains viable as development risk converts into operating risk.

The Infrastructure Capital Landscape

Infrastructure Capital Comes From Different Sources at Different Stages

Infrastructure businesses may access different forms of capital depending on project stage, asset type, contract structure and cash-flow visibility — including public-sector infrastructure lenders, commercial banks, Infrastructure Debt Funds, specialised NBFCs and NaBFID for infrastructure transactions. A typical capital ecosystem may include:

Sponsor Equity

Initial risk capital committed by promoters or project sponsors.

Project-Level Term Debt

Long-term financing structured around project cash flows.

Working Capital & Non-Fund Facilities

Especially important for EPC businesses managing Bank Guarantees, Letters of Credit and mobilisation requirements.

Infrastructure-Specific Institutions

Depending on the transaction and eligibility.

Refinancing Capital

Potentially relevant after construction risk reduces and operations stabilise.

Asset Monetisation

For mature infrastructure assets with established cash flows.

Where Infrastructure Businesses Typically Need Capital

Capital requirements are usually triggered by specific project events.

01

Bid and Development Stage

Before construction begins, sponsors may need to commit resources toward bid preparation, technical studies, development expenditure and equity commitments. At this stage, project risk remains high. The financing question is: how much sponsor capital should be committed before the project reaches financial close?

02

Financial Close

Once a project has contractual and technical clarity, the focus shifts to establishing the capital structure — debt sizing, sponsor equity, security, escrow mechanisms and conditions precedent. Financial close is therefore not simply a documentation milestone. It determines how the project will carry financial obligations through construction and operations.

03

Construction Capital

Infrastructure projects require substantial capital deployment before operating revenue begins. Debt drawdowns are often linked to construction milestones, independent certification and equity infusion. The timing of capital availability must align with the actual construction schedule.

04

EPC Working Capital

For EPC contractors, the financing requirement extends beyond long-term project debt. Contractors may require fund-based working capital, Performance Bank Guarantees, Financial Bank Guarantees, Letters of Credit and Mobilisation Advance facilities. Heavy non-fund bank guarantee requirements are a major financing challenge for infrastructure EPC businesses.

05

Cost Overruns and Delays

Projects rarely operate in a perfect environment. Potential disruptions may include land acquisition delays, right-of-way delays, regulatory delays and construction disruptions. These events can create additional funding requirements. A project may remain economically viable while becoming temporarily financially stressed.

06

Refinancing Mature Assets

Once an infrastructure asset becomes operational, construction risk reduces, cash flows become visible and performance history develops. The financing requirement may change. The original construction-phase capital may no longer be the most appropriate structure.

The Core Infrastructure Financing Challenge

Infrastructure Risk Changes Before Infrastructure Debt Disappears

A project may begin with significant development risk, construction risk and cost risk. After completion, those risks may reduce. But new risks emerge — revenue risk, counterparty risk, operational risk and maintenance obligations. The debt, however, continues.

This creates the central infrastructure financing challenge:

Can the capital structure remain sustainable as the project transitions from construction to operations?

How Lenders Underwrite Infrastructure

What does an infrastructure credit committee actually evaluate? Infrastructure lenders do not evaluate the project based on asset value alone. They evaluate the entire contractual and cash-flow ecosystem surrounding the asset.

1. Contractual Framework

The first question is often: what legally supports the project's future cash flows? Depending on the asset, this may involve concession agreements, annuity arrangements, long-term contracts and government authority obligations. Concessioning authority standing and concession period length are core infrastructure underwriting considerations.

2. Counterparty Quality

The quality of the revenue counterparty matters — government authority, public-sector entity or corporate counterparty. The lender evaluates whether the contractual payer has the capacity and track record to meet its obligations. A strong project agreement is only as bankable as the counterparty behind it.

3. Construction and Completion Risk

Before a project generates revenue, it must be completed. Lenders may therefore examine EPC contractor capability, project timeline, technical feasibility, completion support and cost overrun arrangements. EPC contractor execution history and cost-overrun risk are important parts of infrastructure underwriting.

4. Sponsor Commitment

Infrastructure financing requires clarity on sponsor commitment — how much equity is being contributed, when will equity be infused, and can the sponsor support cost overruns? The lender wants to understand who absorbs the risk if the project does not perform according to the base case.

5. Cash-Flow Visibility

Ultimately, long-term infrastructure debt must be serviced from project cash flows. Lenders analyse revenue assumptions, operating costs, maintenance requirements and debt obligations, to determine whether cash flows can support debt under reasonable stress scenarios.

6. Security and Cash-Flow Control

Infrastructure lenders often require strong control over project cash flows through escrow accounts, Trust and Retention Accounts and controlled payment waterfalls, to ensure that project cash flows follow a defined priority.

The Infrastructure Cash-Flow Waterfall

Revenue Alone Does Not Determine Debt Service Capacity

The sequencing of cash flows matters. A simplified infrastructure cash-flow waterfall:

  1. Project Revenue
  2. Operating Expenses
  3. Maintenance Requirements
  4. Statutory Obligations
  5. Debt Service
  6. Reserve Requirements
  7. Sponsor Distribution

The lender's concern is clear: does the project generate sufficient cash before distributions to support its financial obligations? This is why project-level cash-flow modelling is central to infrastructure financing.

DSCR: The Core Infrastructure Debt Metric

Can the Project Generate Enough Cash to Service Its Debt?

The Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR) measures the relationship between available cash flow and scheduled debt obligations.

DSCR = Cash Available for Debt Service ÷ (Interest + Principal Repayment)

Infrastructure concession-period DSCR considerations are part of project underwriting, with indicative ranges varying by transaction and asset profile. But DSCR should never be treated as an isolated approval threshold. Lenders also examine cash-flow stability, contract strength, construction risk and counterparty quality.

EquiFin Principle

A strong DSCR forecast is only as credible as the assumptions behind the cash flows.

Concession Agreements

The Concession Is Often the Foundation of the Credit Case

For concession-based infrastructure projects, the concession agreement may define project rights, concession period, revenue mechanism, authority obligations and termination provisions. A lender therefore needs to understand: what contractual rights create the project's ability to generate and protect cash flows?

The agreement is not merely a legal document. It can be central to project bankability.

HAM Projects: Understanding the Capital Dynamics

Hybrid Structures Change How Infrastructure Risk Is Shared

Hybrid Annuity Model (HAM) road projects are a key infrastructure transaction scenario. A hybrid structure can change the relationship between construction funding, government support, sponsor capital and long-term annuity cash flows. From a financing perspective, the important questions remain:

  • What portion of project expenditure requires upfront funding?
  • When does cash support become available?
  • What are the conditions for payment?
  • How are annuity flows structured?

The financing structure must reflect the actual timing of contractual cash flows — not just the total project cost.

Right of Way: A Financing Risk, Not Just an Execution Issue

Delayed Land Access Can Become a Capital Structure Problem

Right-of-way delays are frequently viewed as operational challenges. But they can also create financial consequences.

ROW Delay → Construction Delay → Extended Project Timeline → Additional Cost → Delayed Revenue → Financing Pressure

ROW handovers and land acquisition delays are key infrastructure financing risks. This demonstrates an important principle: operational delays eventually appear in the financial model.

EPC Contractors: Infrastructure Finance Is Not Only Project Finance

EPC Businesses Have a Different Capital Requirement

An EPC contractor may have a strong order book. But still experience liquidity pressure. Why? Because the contractor must manage mobilisation, equipment, labour and material procurement before receiving corresponding customer payments. In addition, infrastructure contracts often require substantial performance guarantees, financial guarantees and letters of credit. This creates pressure on banking limits.

The Non-Fund Limit Challenge

A Growing Order Book Can Consume Banking Capacity

Consider an EPC contractor winning multiple projects. Every project may require additional Bank Guarantees, Performance Guarantees and Financial Guarantees. The business may have revenue growth. But its non-fund banking capacity may become constrained. The Non-Fund to Fund-Based Limit Ratio and Order Book-to-Revenue Ratio are relevant infrastructure financial considerations.

The Strategic Question

Does the company's banking structure have enough capacity to support its future order book?

Order Book Is Not the Same as Cash Flow

Contracted Revenue Must Still Be Converted Into Working Capital and Cash

A large order book is positive. But lenders also evaluate execution capability, margin quality, working capital requirement and customer payment behaviour. A contractor may have a ₹1,000 Crore order book, but if margins are thin, receivables are delayed or guarantees consume limits, the business can still experience liquidity pressure.

Infrastructure growth often creates a working capital requirement before it creates operating cash flow.

Project Cost Overruns

Cost Overruns Can Change the Original Financing Case

Project debt is generally structured around an initial project cost, construction timeline and revenue model. If costs increase materially, the original funding plan may no longer be sufficient. The critical questions become:

  • Who funds the additional requirement?
  • Does additional debt remain serviceable?
  • Is additional sponsor equity required?
  • Will the project completion date move?

Lenders want clarity on these questions before approving project debt.

The Role of Technical Independent Review

Financial Models Depend on Physical Project Reality

Infrastructure financing combines engineering, contracts and finance. Lenders may rely on independent technical evaluation to assess construction progress, project cost, completion timelines and technical assumptions. The Lender's Independent Engineer (LIE) and related project documentation are important readiness requirements for infrastructure financing.

EquiFin Perspective

A project financial model cannot be stronger than the engineering assumptions supporting it.

Important Infrastructure Underwriting Metrics

Infrastructure finance requires both financial and operational measurement. Depending on the transaction, lenders may evaluate:

DSCR

Ability of project cash flows to service debt.

Project Cost

Total capital required to complete the asset.

Equity Contribution

Sponsor capital committed to the project.

Construction Progress

Physical execution against planned milestones.

Order Book-to-Revenue Ratio

Particularly relevant for EPC businesses evaluating revenue visibility.

Non-Fund Banking Utilisation

Relevant where guarantees and contingent facilities consume banking capacity.

Debt Maturity Profile

Alignment between debt repayment and project cash-flow generation.

Metrics provide the measurement framework. Contracts and execution determine whether the numbers can be trusted.

Escrow and Trust & Retention Accounts

Cash-Flow Control Is Part of the Financing Structure

In project financing, lenders often require defined mechanisms to control project receipts. A simplified structure may look like:

  1. Project Receipts
  2. Escrow / TRA
  3. Operating Expenses
  4. Debt Servicing
  5. Reserve Accounts
  6. Permitted Distributions

This creates transparency over the movement of project cash flows. The objective is not simply security. It is to establish financial discipline throughout the life of the project.

Debt Tenor Must Follow Project Cash Flow

Short-Term Debt for a Long-Gestation Asset Can Create Structural Stress

Infrastructure assets may take years to construct, commission and stabilise. A repayment profile must therefore be evaluated against the actual operating lifecycle. Key considerations may include moratorium during construction, cash-flow ramp-up and principal repayment schedule.

The central question:

Is the debt schedule aligned with when the project can realistically generate cash?

Refinancing After Stabilisation

Mature Infrastructure Assets Can Enter a Different Capital Market

A completed project with operating history is fundamentally different from an under-construction project. Before completion, the lender evaluates: will the project work? After stabilisation, the lender increasingly evaluates: how reliably does the project generate cash?

This transition may create opportunities for refinancing, longer-tenor debt, portfolio financing and asset monetisation.

EquiFin Perspective

Construction capital should not automatically become permanent capital. Mature assets should periodically reassess whether their financing structure reflects their reduced risk profile.

Frequently Asked Questions

How are infrastructure projects typically financed?

Infrastructure projects may use a combination of sponsor equity, long-term project debt and transaction-specific facilities. The structure depends on project stage, contractual framework, cash-flow visibility and lender assessment of execution risk.

What do lenders evaluate in infrastructure financing?

Lenders typically examine the contractual framework, revenue mechanism, counterparty quality, EPC execution capability, sponsor contribution, project cost, completion risk and projected debt servicing capacity.

What is DSCR in infrastructure finance?

Debt Service Coverage Ratio measures the relationship between cash available for debt servicing and scheduled interest and principal obligations. The appropriate level depends on the asset's risk and cash-flow characteristics.

Why are concession agreements important?

A concession agreement may define project rights, the revenue mechanism, concession period and obligations of the authority and project entity. These provisions can be central to understanding project bankability.

Why do EPC contractors need significant banking limits?

EPC companies may require fund-based working capital as well as non-fund facilities such as performance guarantees, financial guarantees and letters of credit. Growth in the order book can therefore increase banking capacity requirements.

What is the role of an escrow or TRA?

Escrow and Trust & Retention Account structures provide a defined mechanism for receiving and allocating project cash flows, often prioritising operating requirements and debt obligations.

When should an infrastructure asset consider refinancing?

Refinancing may become relevant after construction risk reduces and the asset establishes an operating cash-flow history. The objective is to assess whether the existing financing structure remains appropriate for the asset's current risk profile.
