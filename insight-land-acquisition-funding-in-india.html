Land Acquisition Funding in India: How Developers Finance Land & Development Rights

You have found the land. The seller is ready. The project economics work. Then comes the difficult question: how do you fund the acquisition? Land sits at the earliest and riskiest stage of a project — often before approvals, RERA registration, construction and project cash flows exist. The answer isn't to find a "land loan." It's to structure land acquisition funding as the first stage of the project's overall capital stack.

6 Min Read·Perspective·September 2026

Can Banks Fund Land Acquisition?

For private builders and developers, commercial banks cannot provide fund-based or non-fund-based facilities for the acquisition of land under the applicable RBI framework — even where the land forms part of a housing project. Banks can, however, provide eligible project-linked finance for development and construction once the project reaches that stage.

That distinction matters. A developer asking a bank for ₹50 crore to purchase land is in a fundamentally different conversation from the same developer returning once the land is acquired, approvals are in place, and construction finance is what's needed. The financing options genuinely change as the project moves through its lifecycle.

Can HFCs Fund Land Acquisition?

HFCs shouldn't simply be treated as equivalent to commercial banks here. Depending on the housing project, its structure and the lender's credit policy, land acquisition can form part of an eligible composite or project-financing structure where housing construction is also undertaken.

So the real question isn't "can an HFC fund land?" It's whether this HFC can structure the land acquisition as part of the overall housing or project financing transaction — which depends on the project, the borrower, the security, the approvals in hand, the construction undertaking, and that lender's specific mandate.

Where Does Land Acquisition Capital Actually Come From?

Depending on the transaction, developers typically explore real-estate-focused NBFCs, Housing Finance Companies, private credit funds, Category II AIFs, structured or project-level debt, and promoter or strategic capital. NBFCs are often particularly relevant because the RBI's commercial real-estate framework recognises exposures relating to land acquisition, development and construction — subject to the applicable regulations and the lender's own underwriting policy.

The right lender, in other words, depends on the stage, structure and risk profile of the specific transaction — not on any single "go-to" category of lender.

What Does a Lender Actually Look At?

Land funding is not determined simply by the market value of the property.

Title

Clean, marketable title is fundamental. The lender examines the chain of title, encumbrances, litigation history and the ability to create enforceable security.

Development Potential

Permissible FSI, saleable area, the proposed project, location, development rights and expected project economics.

Promoter

Track record, net worth, existing leverage and experience executing similar projects.

Transaction Structure

Whether the acquisition happens through direct purchase, an SPV, a JDA, development rights, or another structured arrangement can materially change the financing structure available.

Exit

The most important question is usually the simplest one: how will the land-stage debt actually be repaid?

How Much Can a Developer Raise?

There is no universal LTV for land acquisition funding. The fundable amount depends on land value, acquisition price, title quality, location, development potential, promoter contribution, existing leverage, the security structure and the proposed exit — which is why a lender can arrive at a funding number quite different from what the developer initially expects.

The objective shouldn't be to maximise land-stage borrowing. It should be to raise enough capital to complete the acquisition and take the project to its next financing milestone.

The Exit Is the Strategy

Land-stage debt is generally more expensive and carries more development risk than later-stage project finance. That's why the refinancing or repayment strategy needs to be thought through before taking the initial land funding — not after.

The strongest proposals are rarely just "here is the land, and here is how much money I need." They're closer to "here is the acquisition, the development plan, the capital requirement, and the defined path from land acquisition through construction finance to eventual debt repayment."

What Documents Are Usually Required?

A lender will typically want: title documents and the chain of title; a title search or legal due diligence report; encumbrance details; the sale or acquisition agreement; a development agreement or JDA, where applicable; project and development details; FSI and saleable-area assumptions; promoter financials and net worth; existing debt details; project cost and funding plan; the proposed security structure; and an exit or refinancing plan. The quality of this information can materially affect how quickly a transaction gets evaluated.

Why Land Funding Proposals Get Rejected

A good property doesn't automatically make a good credit proposal. The common issues:

  • Unclear title
  • Aggressive land valuation or a high acquisition price
  • Insufficient promoter contribution
  • Unclear development rights
  • Weak project economics
  • Excessive existing leverage
  • No credible exit
  • Approval timelines that don't match the proposed debt tenure
  • Approaching a lender whose mandate doesn't fit the transaction

In many cases, the problem isn't the project itself. It's the funding structure.

EquiFin Perspective

Land acquisition funding is the first step in a broader real-estate capital strategy — what's being acquired, how much capital is actually required, which lender category fits, what security can be created, how the project reaches construction finance, and how the debt ultimately gets repaid. This matters most for land acquisition, development rights, JDA structures, TDR/FSI requirements, premium payments and large urban development projects. The right funding structure is designed around the project's lifecycle — not simply around the amount of money required today.

Key Takeaways

  • Commercial banks cannot fund land acquisition for private builders under the applicable RBI framework.
  • HFCs can take a different approach where land acquisition forms part of an eligible housing or project structure.
  • NBFCs and alternative/private capital are often the more relevant sources for land-stage funding.
  • Title quality, development potential and exit strategy are central to lender underwriting.
  • Land funding should be structured with the next stage of construction or project finance already in mind.
  • The right question isn't "who will fund my land?" — it's what capital structure takes the project from acquisition to construction and eventual repayment.
