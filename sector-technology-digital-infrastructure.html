Technology & Digital Infrastructure

Digital Infrastructure Requires Capital Before Capacity Becomes Revenue.

A data centre may require substantial investment in land, civil infrastructure, substations, electrical systems, cooling and connectivity before a single MW is commercially utilised. But installed capacity alone does not create debt-servicing ability.

The financing case strengthens when infrastructure is supported by reliable power, redundant connectivity, technical efficiency, customer commitments and long-term contracted capacity. EquiFin helps digital infrastructure businesses evaluate capital requirements through the lens of infrastructure readiness, capacity utilisation, customer quality and sustainable debt capacity.

In Digital Infrastructure, Power Creates Capacity. Customers Convert Capacity Into Cash Flow.

A data centre can have land, buildings, data halls, electrical infrastructure and cooling systems. But these alone do not explain its financial value. The operating and financing chain is more complex.

Land & Location → Power Availability → Electrical + Cooling Infrastructure → Commissioned MW Capacity → Customer Commitments → Contracted Capacity → Utilisation → Recurring Revenue → EBITDA → Debt Capacity

Digital infrastructure financing is ultimately about understanding how physical infrastructure becomes contracted, recurring and financeable cash flow.

The Digital Infrastructure Capital Landscape

Capital Requirements Are Driven by Infrastructure Intensity, Not Just Technology Growth

Potential capital providers include global private credit funds, international syndicated loan facilities, private equity credit platforms and domestic commercial banks. Digital infrastructure can require multiple layers of capital.

Infrastructure Project Debt

Potentially relevant for data centre campuses, core infrastructure and large-scale capacity development.

Equipment Financing

Potentially relevant for specialised electrical systems, cooling infrastructure and technical equipment.

Lease Rental Discounting

Potentially relevant for stabilised IT and SEZ assets with contracted rental income — LRD structures on data halls and IT SEZ park LRD refinancing are recognised structures.

Green Financing Structures

Green Bond debt packages are a potential financing structure for relevant infrastructure.

EquiFin Perspective

A digital infrastructure asset passes through several financing milestones. Land acquisition is not bankability. Construction completion is not commercial maturity. Installed MW capacity becomes financially meaningful when it can be supported by reliable infrastructure and contracted demand.

Where Digital Infrastructure Businesses Typically Need Capital

Capital requirements begin well before commercial capacity is utilised.

01

Data Centre Campus Development

Large data centre developments may require capital for land, civil infrastructure, data halls, electrical systems and cooling infrastructure — hyperscale data centre campus civil construction is a recognised transaction scenario. But the financing analysis extends beyond construction cost. The key question becomes: what proportion of future capacity is expected to become commercially contracted — and how quickly?

02

Power Infrastructure and Substations

Digital infrastructure is power intensive. Power substation setup and dual-feed power availability agreements are core transaction and underwriting considerations. Capital may be required for substations, electrical infrastructure and redundant power systems.

Core Financing Insight: for a conventional building, power is an operating utility. For a data centre, power availability is part of the core productive infrastructure.

03

High-Density Server Infrastructure

As technology requirements evolve, digital infrastructure may require investment in higher-density capacity, advanced cooling and supporting electrical systems. This introduces a critical financing consideration: will today's infrastructure remain commercially competitive through the financing period?

04

Capacity Expansion

A digital infrastructure platform may expand by adding new data halls, additional MW capacity and new campuses. Expansion can require significant upfront capital before the additional capacity generates proportional revenue. The capital structure must therefore recognise:

Existing Capacity Cash Flow + New Capacity Capex → Expanded Infrastructure → Future Customer Fill → Incremental EBITDA

05

IT Parks and Technology Real Estate

Technology infrastructure also includes operational real estate supporting technology businesses — IT SEZ park LRD refinancing is a recognised transaction scenario. Where rental income is contracted, the financing lens may shift toward tenant quality, lease tenure, rental visibility and WALE.

06

Refinancing Stabilised Digital Assets

Once infrastructure achieves stable capacity utilisation, established customer contracts and predictable recurring revenue, the capital structure may need to evolve. Potential objectives can include replacing development-stage debt, improving debt tenure, releasing capital and funding the next expansion cycle.

The Core Digital Infrastructure Financing Challenge

Capacity Must Be Built Before It Can Be Sold

This creates a fundamental timing challenge.

Capex Today → Commissioned Capacity → Customer Acquisition → Contracted MW → Revenue Ramp-Up → EBITDA Maturity → Full Debt-Service Capacity

High power infrastructure capex, technology obsolescence cycles and upfront equity requirements before institutional anchor tenant commitments are central financing challenges.

EquiFin Perspective

The financing challenge is not simply funding the build. It is bridging the period between infrastructure investment and contracted capacity maturity.

Signature Insight

How Lenders Underwrite Digital Infrastructure

What does a lender actually evaluate? Four central underwriting considerations stand out: power availability, PUE, anchor hyperscaler commitments and fiber connectivity. These are not isolated technical details. Together, they determine whether a facility is operationally reliable, commercially competitive and capable of attracting institutional customers.

Signature Insight

Power Availability: The First Infrastructure Question

Without Reliable Power, MW Capacity Is Theoretical

Power availability agreements and dual-feed substations are important underwriting considerations. The financing analysis may examine allocated power, reliability, redundancy and infrastructure readiness.

Power Allocation → Grid Access → Substation Infrastructure → Redundancy → Operable Data Centre Capacity

Lender Question

Is the required power infrastructure actually available and operationally dependable for the capacity being financed?

Power Readiness Is Not the Same as Power Requirement

Planned Capacity Must Be Supported by Demonstrable Infrastructure

A business may project future MW capacity. But lenders need to distinguish between planned capacity (intended for future development), installed capacity (infrastructure physically created), commissioned capacity (ready for commercial use) and contracted capacity (committed by customers).

Planned MW → Installed MW → Commissioned MW → Contracted MW → Revenue-Generating MW

Not every MW in a development plan contributes equally to present debt capacity.

Signature Insight

PUE: Infrastructure Efficiency as a Financing Consideration

Power Efficiency Influences Operating Economics

Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE) is a core underwriting metric, with PUE ≤ 1.4 referenced as an indicative benchmark. PUE helps evaluate how efficiently power is used within the data centre environment. The financing relevance is straightforward:

Power Consumption → Operating Cost → Operating Margin → EBITDA → Debt Service Capacity

EquiFin Perspective

In power-intensive infrastructure, technical efficiency eventually becomes a financial variable.

Signature Insight

Contracted Capacity: Where Infrastructure Becomes More Bankable

Installed Capacity Is an Asset. Contracted Capacity Creates Revenue Visibility.

Anchor hyperscaler pre-commitments and Contracted Capacity Fill Rate are central underwriting and financial considerations. Benchmark research references 60% or more contracted capacity prior to sanction as an indicative lender consideration in data-centre transactions.

Total Commissioned MW → Contracted MW → Contracted Capacity % → Revenue Visibility → Credit Comfort

Core Principle

A lender does not only ask how much capacity can be built. The more important question is how much of that capacity already has credible commercial demand.

Why Anchor Hyperscaler Commitments Matter

Customer Quality Can Change the Financing Conversation

Anchor hyperscaler tenant pre-commitments are an important underwriting consideration. An anchor commitment can potentially provide greater visibility into initial capacity utilisation, revenue ramp-up and demand credibility. But the analysis must go beyond the customer name — lenders may examine contract structure, contract tenure, capacity commitment and termination provisions.

EquiFin Perspective

In digital infrastructure, the credit story can move from "build and hope to fill" toward "build against demonstrable demand" when credible capacity commitments exist.

Customer Contracts Are Part of the Infrastructure Credit Case

The Building Is Only One Part of the Security Story

Assignment of long-term hyperscaler master contracts is a potential security and risk-mitigation structure. This reflects an important financing principle:

Physical Asset + Power Infrastructure + Long-Term Customer Contracts = Stronger Revenue Visibility

Key Question

What happens to projected cash flow if a major customer reduces, relocates or terminates contracted capacity?

WALE and Contract Tenure

Long-Term Contracts Can Improve Revenue Visibility

WALE of customer contracts is a relevant financial metric, providing a view of the remaining weighted contractual tenure across customers.

Longer Contract Visibility → More Predictable Revenue Base → Greater Cash Flow Certainty → Stronger Long-Term Financing Case

But WALE alone is insufficient. It should be evaluated alongside customer concentration, counterparty quality and termination rights.

Fiber Connectivity and Redundancy

A Data Centre Is Only as Useful as Its Connectivity

Fiber connectivity access and fiber redundancy agreements are key underwriting and readiness considerations. The lender may need confidence that the facility is supported by adequate connectivity, redundant routes and operational continuity.

EquiFin Perspective

Power keeps digital infrastructure operating. Connectivity makes the infrastructure commercially usable.

Signature Insight

From MW Capacity to Debt Capacity

Capacity Does Not Automatically Equal Revenue

The complete financial chain is:

Commissioned MW → Contracted MW → Customer Utilisation → Recurring Revenue → Operating Cost → EBITDA → Cash Available for Debt Service → Debt Capacity

Central Insight

MW is an infrastructure metric. Contracted MW is a commercial metric. EBITDA is the metric that ultimately determines sustainable debt capacity.

Loan per MW: Connecting Build Cost to Capital Structure

Digital Infrastructure Financing Requires Capacity-Based Debt Discipline

Loan per MW of capacity is a core financial metric and should be benchmarked against build cost.

Total Project Cost → Total Commissioned MW → Capex per MW → Debt per MW → Equity Requirement

The financing objective is not simply to maximise leverage. It is to ensure that the amount of debt per MW remains supportable by the future revenue expected from that MW capacity.

High Upfront Capex: Why Digital Infrastructure Needs Long-Term Capital Thinking

Infrastructure Investment Happens Before Full Revenue Visibility

A data centre may require simultaneous investment in land, civil works, power systems, cooling and connectivity before customer utilisation reaches maturity. This creates a capital gap:

High Initial Capex → Low Initial Utilisation → Ramp-Up Period → Contracted Capacity Growth → Recurring EBITDA

The financing structure must recognise this timing difference. A capital structure designed only around construction completion may fail to recognise the commercial ramp-up required after commissioning.

Technology Obsolescence: A Different Type of Infrastructure Risk

Long-Term Debt Meets Rapid Technology Change

Traditional infrastructure assets can sometimes operate with relatively stable technology for extended periods. Digital infrastructure faces a different challenge — technology obsolescence cycles and changes in server power density are recognised sector risks. The financing question becomes: will the infrastructure remain commercially relevant through the life of the capital raised against it? This may affect future capex, upgrade requirements and asset competitiveness.

High-Density Computing and Infrastructure Adaptability

Customer Requirements Can Change Faster Than Traditional Infrastructure Cycles

Changes in server power density are a recognised risk consideration. A financing model must therefore recognise the possibility that future customer requirements may demand higher power density, enhanced cooling and additional infrastructure upgrades.

EquiFin Perspective

Digital infrastructure must be evaluated not only for what it can support today, but for how adaptable the asset is to changing technical requirements.

Data Centre Operating Risk: Infrastructure Failure Can Become Revenue Risk

Reliability Is Part of the Commercial Product

Grid power interruptions and cooling infrastructure failure are key sector risks. The financial chain is direct:

Infrastructure Failure → Service Disruption → Customer Risk → Contract Pressure → Revenue Impact

Key Principle

For digital infrastructure, operational resilience is not merely an engineering consideration. It is part of the long-term revenue protection framework.

Customer Concentration and Client Churn

Contracted Revenue Must Still Be Tested for Concentration Risk

A facility may have strong contracted capacity. But lenders may evaluate whether a significant proportion depends on one customer, a limited group of customers, or short-duration contracts. Tenant contract terminations and client churn to lower-cost providers are recognised sector risks.

Lender Question

How quickly can contracted revenue decline if a major customer exits?

Data Centre Utilisation Ramp-Up

Commissioning Capacity and Filling Capacity Are Different Milestones

Construction → Commissioning → Technical Readiness → Anchor Customer → Initial Contracted MW → Capacity Fill-Up → Stabilised Utilisation

The capital structure must recognise that each stage carries different risk:

Development Risk

Can the infrastructure be completed?

Operational Risk

Can the facility operate reliably?

Commercial Risk

Can the capacity be contracted?

Financial Risk

Can recurring cash flow support the debt?

IT Park and Technology Real Estate Financing

Not All Digital Infrastructure Revenue Is Capacity-Based

Technology parks and IT SEZ assets may operate around leased commercial space — IT SEZ park LRD refinancing is a recognised transaction. In such cases, the financing lens may focus more heavily on tenant quality, lease contracts, rental income and WALE.

Leased Area → Contracted Rent → Net Operating Income → Cash Flow Visibility → LRD Capacity

This creates a different underwriting model from a data centre.

Technology real estate and digital infrastructure may both serve the technology economy, but their cash-flow engines are fundamentally different. The financing structure must reflect that difference.

DSCR and Cash Flow Protection

Ultimately, Debt Must Be Supported by Cash Flow

While sector research emphasises Loan/MW, PUE and contracted capacity metrics, the broader financing structure must ultimately assess the relationship between operating cash flow and scheduled debt obligations.

DSCR = Cash Available for Debt Service ÷ (Interest + Principal Repayment)

The sector-specific research does not prescribe a single universal DSCR threshold for digital infrastructure. EquiFin therefore avoids presenting an artificial benchmark and instead positions DSCR around the specific contract structure, capacity maturity, customer profile and debt tenure — important for maintaining institutional credibility.

Security Structure in Digital Infrastructure Financing

The Credit Structure Can Extend Beyond Physical Real Estate

Potential security and mitigation structures include first charge on data centre real estate, charge on electrical equipment, and assignment of long-term hyperscaler master contracts.

Physical Security (Real Estate) + Movable Infrastructure (Electrical Systems) + Contractual Cash Flow (Customer Agreements) = Financing Security Package

Important Insight

The strongest infrastructure financing cases connect physical asset security with the underlying contracts that generate cash flow from those assets.

Capital Structure: Development Debt vs Stabilised Debt

The Same Asset Can Have Different Financing Profiles at Different Stages

Development Stage

Primary considerations: construction, power readiness, equity contribution.

Commercial Ramp-Up

Primary considerations: customer commitments, capacity fill.

Stabilised Operations

Primary considerations: recurring revenue, contract tenure, EBITDA.

Development (High Execution Risk) → Ramp-Up (High Commercial Risk) → Stabilised (Higher Cash Flow Visibility)

EquiFin Perspective

Capital should evolve with the asset. Development-stage infrastructure and stabilised contracted infrastructure should not automatically carry the same financing assumptions.

Refinancing Stabilised Digital Infrastructure

Operating Maturity Can Create a New Capital Conversation

Once an asset demonstrates stable operations, reliable power infrastructure, contracted customers and consistent capacity utilisation, management may evaluate refinancing. Potential objectives can include replacing higher-cost capital, extending tenure, funding further capacity and improving capital efficiency.

The key question becomes:

Does the current debt structure reflect the demonstrated maturity and recurring cash-flow visibility of the asset?

Important Digital Infrastructure Underwriting Metrics

Sector metrics connect engineering capacity with financial capacity.

Loan per MW

Measures debt relative to commissioned infrastructure capacity. Should be benchmarked against build cost.

Contracted Capacity Fill Rate

Measures the proportion of available capacity supported by customer commitments. 60%+ pre-sanction contracted capacity is an indicative benchmark.

PUE

Measures power efficiency. PUE ≤ 1.4 is an indicative benchmark.

WALE of Customer Contracts

Helps evaluate weighted contractual revenue visibility.

Commissioned vs Contracted MW

Helps distinguish physical infrastructure readiness from commercial maturity.

Important Principle — digital infrastructure metrics must connect technical performance with commercial utilisation. MW without contracts, contracts without reliable infrastructure and efficiency without revenue do not independently establish debt capacity.

Frequently Asked Questions

How are data centres financed in India?

Data centre financing may involve infrastructure project debt, equipment financing, private credit, syndicated facilities and, in appropriate cases, refinancing structures based on stabilised contracted cash flows. LRD structures and green bond debt packages are also potential financing formats.

What do lenders evaluate before financing a data centre?

Core considerations include power availability, dual-feed infrastructure, PUE, anchor hyperscaler commitments and fiber connectivity. Lenders may also evaluate contracted capacity, customer contract tenure and infrastructure readiness.

Why is power availability important in data centre financing?

Power is part of the productive infrastructure of a data centre. Without reliable and adequate power infrastructure, commissioned capacity cannot operate at the intended level.

What is PUE?

PUE, or Power Usage Effectiveness, is a measure used to evaluate power efficiency in a data centre environment. It is a core underwriting metric, with approximately 1.4 or below often referenced as an indicative benchmark.

What is contracted capacity?

Contracted capacity refers to infrastructure capacity committed by customers under commercial arrangements. It provides a clearer view of revenue visibility than installed capacity alone.

Why do hyperscaler commitments matter to lenders?

Anchor customer commitments can provide evidence of demand and visibility into the future utilisation of commissioned infrastructure. The contract structure and tenure remain important to the underwriting analysis.

What is Loan per MW?

Loan per MW relates the amount of financing to the infrastructure capacity being developed. It is a core metric that should be benchmarked against build cost.

Can a stabilised data centre be refinanced?

Once an asset has demonstrated reliable operations, customer contracts and recurring cash flow, refinancing may be evaluated to improve the alignment between capital structure and asset maturity.

Digital Infrastructure Capital Must Bridge the Gap Between Capacity and Contracted Revenue.

The largest financing challenge in digital infrastructure is often the period between capital deployment and commercial maturity. Power infrastructure must be created. Capacity must be commissioned. Customers must be contracted. Revenue must become recurring. Whether the requirement involves a new data centre campus, power infrastructure, capacity expansion or refinancing, the capital structure must recognise both the engineering reality of the asset and the commercial pathway to sustainable cash flow.
