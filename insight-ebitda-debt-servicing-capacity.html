Why EBITDA Is Not the Same as Debt Servicing Capacity

A company may report healthy EBITDA and still face constraints in raising or servicing debt. The reason is that operating profitability is only one part of the journey from business performance to actual cash available for financial obligations.

9 Min Read·Perspective·September 2026

The Question

A company reports strong financial performance. Revenue is growing. EBITDA margins are healthy. Profitability has improved over the last several years. Management believes the business is well positioned to raise additional debt. Yet the lender remains cautious.

The reason is often found in a distinction that is important but frequently overlooked: EBITDA is not the same as cash available to service debt. EBITDA is an important measure of operating performance — it helps investors, lenders and management understand the earnings generated by the core operations of a business before considering interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation. But debt is not serviced with EBITDA. Interest and principal repayments ultimately require cash. Between reported EBITDA and actual cash available for debt servicing, a business may need to fund working capital, capital expenditure, taxes, existing interest obligations, principal repayments and other financial commitments. This difference can materially influence how much debt a company can prudently absorb.

Why This Distinction Matters

In financing discussions, EBITDA is often one of the first numbers mentioned. A company may say, “we generate ₹100 crore of EBITDA and therefore have capacity to raise additional debt.” That statement may be directionally relevant, but it is incomplete. Consider two companies that both generate ₹100 crore of EBITDA. Company A operates an asset-light business with limited capital expenditure, stable receivables, strong operating cash conversion and low maintenance requirements. Company B operates in a capital-intensive sector with significant working capital requirements, regular capital expenditure, long receivable cycles and high inventory requirements. Their EBITDA is identical. Their actual capacity to service additional debt may not be. This is why EBITDA should be viewed as an important starting point for analysis—not the final answer.

From EBITDA to Debt Servicing: What Happens in Between?

A useful way to think about debt servicing capacity is to follow the journey from operating profitability to available cash. The exact analysis will vary by business and transaction, but the underlying principle remains important: not all EBITDA becomes freely available cash.

How Lenders May Look Beyond EBITDA

When evaluating a corporate borrowing proposal, lenders may consider several layers of financial analysis, attempting to move beyond the headline earnings number to understand the full financial picture.

  • Layer 01 — Historical Performance: Revenue trends, EBITDA margins, profitability and operating performance.
  • Layer 02 — Cash Conversion: Receivable movements, inventory, payables and operating cash flow.
  • Layer 03 — Capital Requirements: Maintenance capex, growth capex and project expenditure.
  • Layer 04 — Existing Financial Obligations: Interest, principal repayments, lease commitments and other debt obligations.
  • Layer 05 — Future Capacity: Projected cash flows, incremental obligations and sensitivity to downside scenarios.

01 — Working Capital Can Absorb Significant Cash

A growing business can be profitable and still experience pressure on liquidity. One of the most common reasons is working capital. Consider a manufacturing company experiencing rapid growth — revenue increases, production increases, sales improve, EBITDA rises. But growth may also require higher inventory, larger raw material purchases, higher receivables and increased operating balances. As a result, cash becomes tied up in the operating cycle, and the company may report higher profitability while simultaneously requiring additional bank funding. This is not necessarily a sign of poor performance — it reflects the difference between accounting profitability and cash conversion. The question is not only how much EBITDA the company generates; it is also how much of that EBITDA converts into cash after supporting the operating cycle.

02 — Capital Expenditure Requires Cash Before It Generates Returns

Many businesses require regular investment to maintain and grow operations — plant and machinery, manufacturing facilities, warehouses, technology infrastructure, hotels, healthcare facilities and renewable energy assets. The EBITDA generated by a business may therefore need to be partially reinvested. There are generally two broad considerations: maintenance capex, the capital required to maintain existing operating capability, and growth capex, capital deployed to expand future capacity. Both can affect the amount of internally generated cash available for financial obligations. When evaluating borrowing capacity, it is therefore important to understand how much capital the business needs to reinvest simply to sustain and grow its operations.

03 — Taxes Are a Real Cash Outflow

EBITDA is calculated before taxes. Debt servicing capacity is not. Depending on the company’s tax position, cash taxes can materially reduce available cash. Historical financial statements may show accounting tax expenses, but transaction analysis may also consider actual cash tax payments, deferred tax positions, tax incentives and changes in tax obligations over time. The purpose is not to create a universal formula — it is to recognise that EBITDA represents an earlier point in the cash-flow journey.

04 — Existing Interest Must Be Paid Before New Debt Is Considered

A company seeking incremental debt already has financial obligations — term loan interest, working capital interest, NBFC borrowings, lease liabilities and other financing costs. Additional debt increases the financial burden, so incremental borrowing should generally be assessed alongside the company’s existing obligations. The relevant question becomes: after servicing existing debt, how much capacity remains? This is particularly important when interest rates rise or when a company has accumulated multiple layers of borrowing over time — a business may appear to have manageable leverage based on historical EBITDA, but a substantial increase in interest cost can reduce financial flexibility more quickly than expected.

05 — Principal Repayment Is Different From Interest

Interest expense appears in the profit and loss account. Principal repayment generally does not reduce EBITDA or operating profit — but it still requires cash. This is one of the reasons why profit-based analysis alone may not fully capture debt servicing pressure. Consider two loans of the same amount: one with a longer tenor and gradual repayment, another with a shorter tenor and aggressive amortisation. The interest cost may be broadly comparable in the early stages, but the annual principal repayment requirement can be materially different. For the borrower, this difference affects liquidity; for the lender, it affects the assessment of repayment capacity. This is why the structure of debt matters alongside the amount of debt.

06 — Growth Can Increase EBITDA and Reduce Free Cash Flow at the Same Time

This is one of the more important financial dynamics for growing businesses. Growth is usually associated with positive momentum. However, growth can also consume capital. Imagine a company that increases revenue by 40% — to support that growth, it may require additional inventory, higher receivables, more employees, new equipment and additional facilities. The business is performing well operationally, but the cash requirement of growth may exceed internally generated funds. This is why rapidly growing companies can experience financing pressure despite strong EBITDA performance. For promoters and CFOs, the implication is important: growth strategy and capital planning need to be considered together.

Why Financial Ratios Need Context

Ratios are useful tools in corporate finance — common measures include Debt/EBITDA, interest coverage, DSCR and Net Debt/EBITDA. These ratios can help compare financial obligations against earnings or cash-flow measures. However, ratios should be interpreted within the context of the business. Two companies may report the same Debt/EBITDA ratio, but one may have stable contracted revenues, low working capital intensity and predictable cash flows, while the other has cyclical revenues, significant receivable concentration and high reinvestment requirements. The numerical ratio may look similar; the underlying financial risk may not be. The objective of analysis should therefore be to understand the economic reality behind the ratio — not simply to achieve a particular ratio.

The Importance of Cash-Flow Timing

The amount of cash generated is important. The timing of cash generation can be equally important. Consider a business that generates significant annual cash flow — if most of that cash is received during the final quarter of the financial year but debt repayments are heavily concentrated earlier, the company may still experience liquidity pressure. This creates a timing mismatch. Seasonal businesses, project businesses, construction companies and hospitality businesses may experience uneven cash-flow patterns, and debt structures that ignore these patterns can create unnecessary pressure. This is why debt servicing analysis should consider how much cash is generated—and when it is generated.

A Simplified Illustration

Consider two companies, both reporting ₹100 crore of EBITDA. Company A has a working capital requirement of ₹10 crore, maintenance capex of ₹10 crore, existing interest of ₹15 crore and limited other major cash requirements — the business retains relatively strong cash availability. Company B has a working capital requirement of ₹35 crore, maintenance and growth capex of ₹30 crore, existing interest of ₹15 crore and significant other cash requirements. Both companies report the same EBITDA, but their financial flexibility can be materially different. The illustration is intentionally simplified — actual analysis depends on the specific business and transaction — but the broader point remains: the quality of EBITDA is influenced by what happens to the cash after EBITDA is generated.

What This Means for Incremental Borrowing

Before taking additional debt, businesses may benefit from looking beyond the maximum amount a lender may be willing to provide. A more strategic analysis asks: what level of debt can the business comfortably support? The word comfortably matters, because the answer should ideally consider more than the base-case financial model. Management may also evaluate delayed revenue, margin pressure, higher working capital requirements, capex overruns and increased interest costs. The objective is not to eliminate all financial risk — that is rarely possible. It is to understand the relationship between business performance and financial obligations before additional leverage is introduced.

The Transaction Structure Can Improve or Reduce Financial Pressure

Suppose a company requires capital for an asset that will generate cash over ten years. A short repayment structure may create significant annual cash pressure. A longer structure aligned with the asset’s cash generation may produce a different financial profile. Similarly, if a company requires temporary capital to support a short operating cycle, excessively long-term financing may not be the most efficient structure. The key principle is that capital should be structured with reference to the economic characteristics of the underlying requirement — considering tenor, moratorium, repayment schedule, interest servicing, seasonal cash flows and project implementation timeline. This is why debt capacity and debt structure cannot be analysed independently.

EquiFin Perspective

EBITDA is an important indicator of operating performance, but debt is serviced through cash flows. Understanding the journey between the two is central to evaluating sustainable borrowing capacity.

Key Takeaways

  • EBITDA and debt servicing capacity are related, but they are not the same thing.
  • Working capital can absorb significant cash, particularly during periods of rapid growth.
  • Maintenance and growth capital expenditure can materially affect cash available for financial obligations.
  • Existing interest and principal repayments must be considered before evaluating incremental debt capacity.
  • The timing of cash flows is as important as the total amount generated.
  • Financial ratios provide useful indicators but should be interpreted in the context of the underlying business.
  • A strong EBITDA number does not automatically mean a business can comfortably absorb additional leverage.
  • Debt capacity should be evaluated alongside debt structure, repayment timing and downside resilience.

Closing Perspective

EBITDA is often a convenient starting point because it provides a simple measure of operating performance. But financing decisions require a deeper view. Between EBITDA generation and actual debt repayment lies the economic reality of the business: cash tied up in operations, capital required to maintain growth, existing financial commitments and the timing of cash generation.

For promoters and CFOs, understanding this difference can materially improve capital planning. The question is not simply how much EBITDA the company generates. The more relevant question is: after supporting the business and meeting existing commitments, how much reliable cash remains available to service additional financial obligations? That distinction is where operating performance becomes financial capacity.
