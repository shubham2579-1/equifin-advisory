Project Finance

Long-Term Project Capital Starts With Bankable Cash Flows.

A project's ability to raise debt depends on more than its total project cost or the value of underlying assets. Lenders evaluate how the project will be built, when it will begin operating and whether future cash flows can reliably service long-term debt.

EquiFin helps sponsors structure project financing around project economics, construction risk, repayment capacity and lender underwriting requirements.

Project Finance Is Not Simply a Term Loan for Capital Expenditure

Large projects consume capital before they generate revenue. That creates a financing challenge that conventional corporate borrowing does not always address adequately.

A project may require several years of construction, phased capital deployment, regulatory approvals, specialised contractors and a gradual operational ramp-up. Debt therefore has to be structured around the lifecycle of the asset — not simply sanctioned as a fixed amount and repaid immediately.

The central question for a project lender is: can the project generate sufficient and sufficiently reliable cash flows to service debt once it becomes operational? This is why project finance requires deeper work around:

  • Project cost
  • Means of finance
  • Construction schedule
  • Revenue assumptions
  • Operating costs
  • Debt sizing
  • Moratorium
  • Repayment profile
  • DSCR
  • Security and cash-flow controls

EquiFin approaches project finance as a bankability and transaction-structuring exercise — not simply a search for a project loan.

Project Capital Must Match the Lifecycle of the Asset

A project's financing requirement changes as it moves from development to construction and eventually operations. The capital structure that works during construction may not be appropriate once the asset is generating stable operating cash flows. A typical project lifecycle may involve:

Development Stage — Project concept, feasibility, approvals and preliminary expenditure.

Financial Structuring Stage — Project cost estimation, promoter contribution, debt sizing and lender engagement.

Construction Stage — Phased deployment of debt and equity against project milestones.

Commissioning Stage — Completion testing, regulatory approvals and preparation for operations.

Operational Ramp-Up — Revenue generation begins, but utilisation may take time to stabilise.

Stabilised Operations — The project generates more predictable cash flows and services long-term debt.

The financing structure should recognise these transitions.

Where Businesses Typically Need Project Finance

01

Greenfield Projects

A new project has no operating history of its own. Lenders therefore need to rely on:

  • Sponsor capability
  • Technical feasibility
  • Project economics
  • Construction arrangements
  • Revenue visibility
  • Financial projections

The challenge is to demonstrate that the project can move from a development concept to a self-sustaining operating asset.

02

Brownfield Expansion

An existing business may be expanding capacity through a new production line, additional facility or major asset investment. While an operating track record can provide comfort, the expansion still needs to be evaluated independently. Key questions include:

  • Is additional capacity required?
  • How quickly will the new capacity be utilised?
  • Will the existing business support the project during ramp-up?
  • Does the incremental cash flow justify the proposed debt?
03

Infrastructure Development

Infrastructure projects often involve long development periods and complex contractual arrangements. Financing considerations may include:

  • Concession arrangements
  • Construction timelines
  • Counterparty obligations
  • Traffic or utilisation assumptions
  • Government counterparties
  • Escrow mechanisms
  • Long-term cash-flow visibility
04

Renewable Energy Projects

Renewable projects are typically evaluated against their ability to generate predictable energy output and contracted revenue. Important considerations can include:

  • Project configuration
  • Construction completion
  • Power purchase arrangements
  • Counterparty quality
  • Generation assumptions
  • Grid connectivity
  • Cash-flow coverage
05

Industrial & Manufacturing Projects

Large industrial projects require alignment between capital expenditure, capacity creation, working capital, production ramp-up, customer demand and operating margins. A project may be technically complete but still take time to reach intended utilisation. Debt servicing needs to account for that transition.

06

Asset-Intensive Expansion

Projects involving significant physical assets often require long-term capital because the economic life of the asset extends beyond a conventional short-tenor corporate borrowing period. The financing question becomes: does the debt tenor reasonably reflect the period over which the asset will generate cash flows?

Why a Technically Viable Project May Still Struggle to Raise Debt

A commercially attractive project is not automatically a bankable project. Lenders examine whether the assumptions supporting the project can withstand execution delays and operating downside.

Construction Delays

A delay can create multiple financial consequences simultaneously. Construction costs may increase while revenue generation is postponed, while interest during construction may continue to accumulate. A delay therefore affects total project cost, debt requirement, commissioning date, cash-flow commencement and the debt servicing timeline.

Cost Overruns

Initial project costs are rarely the only consideration. Lenders also assess contingency provisions, cost overrun commitments, availability of additional sponsor support, sensitivity to delays and price escalation risk. The financing structure needs clarity on who absorbs a cost overrun if the original project budget proves insufficient.

Revenue Assumptions

Projected revenue is often central to debt sizing. However, lenders differentiate between contracted revenue, visible demand, management assumptions, merchant exposure and long-term customer arrangements. The stronger and more predictable the cash-flow visibility, the more robust the credit case may become.

Ramp-Up Risk

Commissioning is not the same as stabilisation. A plant may begin operations but operate below intended capacity during the initial period. Similarly, an infrastructure or energy asset may require a period before operating performance becomes predictable. This transition is frequently underestimated during project structuring.

Mismatch Between Construction and Debt Drawdown

Project capital is generally deployed in phases. Debt disbursement must therefore align with construction milestones, equity infusion, certification requirements, conditions precedent and lender monitoring. A sanctioned facility that cannot be drawn efficiently against the project's execution schedule can create liquidity pressure.

How Do Lenders Evaluate a Project Finance Transaction?

Project lenders primarily evaluate the economics and bankability of the underlying asset. The sponsor's financial strength matters, particularly during construction and in relation to support obligations, but the project itself must demonstrate a credible path towards generating sufficient cash flows to service debt. The underwriting framework generally combines technical, financial, contractual and execution considerations.

1. Techno-Economic Viability

A TEV assessment examines whether the project's technical configuration and economic assumptions are credible.

Depending on the transaction, lenders may assess project technology, capacity assumptions, capital cost, operating cost, construction timeline, revenue projections, demand assumptions and financial viability. The purpose is not merely to validate a project report — it is to test whether the assumptions used to size debt are sufficiently robust.

2. Debt Service Coverage Ratio

Can project cash flows service scheduled debt?

The Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR) compares the cash available for debt service against scheduled principal and interest obligations. Project lenders typically examine both minimum DSCR and average DSCR across the debt tenor. Benchmark ranges are often illustratively in the region of approximately 1.25x–1.40x depending on the transaction and risk profile, though actual requirements vary by sector, lender and project characteristics. A project's headline IRR may appear attractive while its cash flows remain insufficiently resilient to support the proposed amortisation schedule.

3. Project Completion Risk

Lenders need confidence that the asset can be completed within the expected cost and timeline. They may assess EPC contractor credentials, construction experience, contract structure, completion guarantees, liquidated damages provisions, contingency and sponsor support. The construction phase is often the period of highest uncertainty.

4. Revenue & Offtake Risk

How the project generates revenue matters significantly. Depending on the sector, lenders may examine Power Purchase Agreements, concession agreements, take-or-pay contracts, long-term customer arrangements, traffic assumptions, capacity utilisation and counterparty credit quality. The question is not simply "how much revenue can the project generate?" — it is "how predictable and enforceable is that revenue?"

5. Promoter Contribution

Project lenders generally expect sponsors to retain meaningful financial commitment. Promoter contribution demonstrates alignment and provides a buffer against over-reliance on debt. Lenders may assess the quantum of equity, timing of equity infusion, source of promoter contribution, subordination arrangements and ability to support contingencies.

6. Security & Cash-Flow Controls

Project financing often relies on a defined security package and mechanisms to control project cash flows. Depending on the transaction, this may involve charge over project assets, assignment of project contracts, escrow arrangements, a Trust and Retention Account (TRA), charge over receivables and sponsor support arrangements. The objective is to create visibility and discipline around how project cash flows are received and applied.

7. Conditions Precedent

A loan sanction does not automatically mean immediate disbursement. Before funds can be released, lenders may require the satisfaction of Conditions Precedent (CPs), which can include regulatory approvals, equity infusion, security creation, execution of key agreements, insurance, technical certifications and legal documentation. The ability to move efficiently from sanction to first disbursement is therefore an important execution consideration.

Structuring Debt Around Project Economics

The appropriate financing structure depends on the nature of the asset and the predictability of its cash flows.

Greenfield Project Debt

Typically structured around:

  • Total project cost
  • Promoter contribution
  • Construction period
  • Interest during construction
  • Moratorium
  • Long-term amortisation

Brownfield Expansion Finance

May consider existing operating performance, incremental project cash flows, capacity utilisation and integration with existing operations.

Consortium Financing

Larger projects may exceed the exposure appetite of a single institution. The transaction may therefore involve multiple lenders with considerations around lead lender coordination, common financing documentation, pari-passu security, inter-creditor arrangements and shared monitoring.

Sector-Specific Project Financing

Project structures may differ materially across renewable energy, infrastructure, manufacturing, logistics, hospitality and healthcare assets. The underlying principle remains consistent: the debt structure should reflect the project's actual cash-flow profile and risk characteristics.

Key Project Finance Components

Project Cost

A comprehensive estimate of the capital required to develop and commission the project. Depending on the project, this may include:

  • Land
  • Civil works
  • Plant & machinery
  • EPC cost
  • Preliminary expenses
  • Interest during construction
  • Contingencies
  • Financing costs

Means of Finance

The proposed combination of promoter equity, senior debt and other eligible capital sources. The objective is to ensure an appropriate balance between sponsor risk and lender exposure.

Moratorium

A moratorium can provide a period before scheduled principal repayment begins. For projects with a construction and ramp-up period, this can be important because principal repayment should not necessarily begin before the asset has established sufficient cash generation.

Debt Amortisation

Amortisation refers to the scheduled repayment of principal over the life of the facility. A project finance repayment profile should be evaluated against projected cash flows — not simply divided into uniform instalments by default.

Financial Close

Financial close is the point at which financing documentation and required conditions are sufficiently completed for the transaction to move into execution. It represents a major transaction milestone, but project execution continues through construction and disbursement monitoring.

Commercial Operation Date

The Commercial Operation Date (COD) generally marks the point at which a project is considered operational under the relevant contractual or project framework. However, COD does not necessarily mean that the project has reached full financial stabilisation — that distinction matters when structuring the transition from construction financing to scheduled debt servicing.

Execution may involve term sheet discussions, documentation, security perfection, CP fulfilment, equity infusion, technical monitoring and milestone-based disbursements — supporting the transaction through to the point at which committed capital can actually be deployed.

Important Project Finance Considerations

The metrics and assumptions behind project debt:

DSCR

Measures the relationship between cash available for debt servicing and scheduled debt obligations. Both minimum and average DSCR can be relevant.

LLCR

The Loan Life Coverage Ratio (LLCR) provides an additional perspective on the relationship between the present value of project cash flows and outstanding debt over the loan life. It is particularly relevant in longer-duration project financing structures.

Debt-Equity Mix

The proportion of debt relative to sponsor capital influences project leverage, lender risk, equity returns and downside resilience. Higher leverage can improve equity efficiency but reduces the financial buffer available to absorb project underperformance.

Project Cost Contingency

Projects should account for uncertainty. An inadequate contingency provision can result in a funding gap if costs increase or execution is delayed.

Construction Period

The financing structure needs to reflect realistic — not merely optimistic — construction timelines. An underestimated completion period can create pressure on interest during construction, project cost, moratorium and COD assumptions.

Ramp-Up Assumptions

Initial operations may not immediately reflect stabilised performance. Utilisation, production efficiency or customer adoption can take time to mature.

Cash-Flow Waterfall

Where applicable, lenders may require a defined priority for how project cash flows are applied:

  1. Operating expenses
  2. Statutory obligations
  3. Debt servicing
  4. Reserve requirements
  5. Other permitted distributions

The precise structure depends on transaction documentation.

Signature Insight

From Construction to Operations: The Critical Transition

One of the most important periods in a project financing transaction is the transition between physical completion and stable operations. Construction may be substantially complete, but:

  • Production may still be ramping up
  • Revenue may remain below projections
  • Working capital requirements may emerge
  • Operational inefficiencies may need to be resolved

At the same time, the debt structure may begin moving towards scheduled repayment. This creates a potential liquidity gap.

A well-considered project finance structure should therefore examine not only:

When will the project be completed?

but also:

When will the project be capable of comfortably servicing debt?

EquiFin Perspective

Project completion and project bankability are not the same thing. A project can be technically sound, fully approved and commercially attractive, yet remain difficult to finance if the construction timeline, revenue assumptions and debt repayment profile do not leave sufficient room for downside. Strong project finance structures are built around resilience — not only base-case projections.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is project finance?

Project finance is a financing approach in which the proposed project's economics, assets and future cash flows play a central role in determining debt capacity and repayment. The structure is commonly used for capital-intensive projects with defined development, construction and operating phases.

How is project debt sized?

Project debt is typically assessed against factors such as total project cost, promoter contribution, projected cash flows and the project's ability to meet scheduled debt obligations. Coverage metrics such as DSCR may form an important part of debt sizing.

What is a DSCR in project finance?

Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR) measures the relationship between cash available for debt service and scheduled principal and interest obligations. Lenders may evaluate both minimum and average DSCR over the financing period.

What is a TEV study?

A Techno-Economic Viability (TEV) assessment examines the technical and economic assumptions supporting a project. Depending on the transaction, it may review areas such as technology, project cost, construction timelines, operating assumptions, revenue projections and overall financial viability.

What is promoter contribution in project finance?

Promoter contribution refers broadly to the capital that sponsors commit to the project. Lenders generally evaluate both the quantum and timing of sponsor capital because it demonstrates financial alignment and provides a buffer against excessive reliance on debt.

Why is a moratorium important for a project loan?

Projects often require a period of construction and operational ramp-up before they generate stable cash flows. A moratorium can defer principal repayment for an agreed period, helping align the commencement of debt servicing with the project's ability to generate cash.

What are Conditions Precedent in project finance?

Conditions Precedent are requirements that generally need to be fulfilled before a lender permits disbursement. Depending on the transaction, they can include approvals, equity contribution, security creation, documentation and technical or legal requirements.

Does project finance guarantee that a project will receive funding?

No. EquiFin provides advisory and transaction execution support. Financing remains subject to the independent appraisal, credit processes, documentation requirements and approvals of the relevant capital providers.
