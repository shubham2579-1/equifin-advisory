How Promoters Should Think About Capital Structure During Growth

Growth creates a demand for capital. The more important strategic question is not simply where that capital can be raised, but how each financing decision changes ownership, leverage, cash-flow obligations and future financial flexibility.

10 Min Read·Perspective·September 2026

The Question

A business is growing. Revenue opportunities are increasing. Capacity needs to expand. New projects are being considered. Working capital requirements are rising. Management now needs additional capital. At this point, a familiar question emerges: should we raise debt or equity? It is an important question, but it is often framed too narrowly.

Capital structure is not simply a choice between debt and equity. It is a broader question about how a business finances its growth while balancing ownership, financial risk, cash-flow obligations, cost of capital, control and future flexibility. A company can raise capital successfully and still create a difficult capital structure — it may raise too much short-term debt, dilute equity earlier than necessary, use working capital to fund long-term assets, pledge strategic assets without considering future requirements, or accumulate multiple layers of financing without a clear view of the combined repayment burden. The challenge is therefore not only accessing capital. It is designing a financial foundation capable of supporting the next stage of the business.

Growth Changes the Capital Question

At an early stage, a promoter may primarily think about access to capital — how do we fund the next opportunity? As the business grows, the question becomes more complex. Capital decisions begin to interact: a term loan affects leverage, a working capital facility affects liquidity, an equity raise affects ownership, a project investment affects future cash flows, and a security package affects future borrowing flexibility. Over time, these individual decisions become a capital structure, and the quality of that structure can influence how confidently a company is able to pursue its next opportunity.

Capital Structure Is About More Than Debt and Equity

At its simplest, capital structure describes how a business is financed. But from a management perspective, the more useful view is to consider multiple sources of capital — internal accruals, which is capital generated and retained by the business; debt, which creates contractual repayment obligations; equity, which provides risk-bearing funding but affects ownership and economic participation; and, in certain situations, hybrid or structured capital that sits between traditional debt and equity. Each source has a different economic behaviour. The relevant question is not which source of capital is universally better — there is no universal answer — it is which form of capital is appropriate for this requirement, at this stage of the business, given its cash flows and future plans.

01 — Start With the Purpose of Capital

Before deciding whether to use debt or equity, management should first define what the capital is intended to do. Working capital supports inventory, receivables and the operating cycle. Capacity expansion involves capital invested into plant, machinery or facilities expected to generate returns over several years. Acquisition capital is used to acquire another business or strategic asset. Project development capital is deployed during a development and implementation cycle before operations begin. Strategic transformation capital is invested into technology, new markets or business capabilities where returns may take longer to emerge. A short operating requirement and a long-term strategic investment do not necessarily need the same capital solution — capital structure should begin with the economic purpose of capital, not the availability of a particular financing product.

02 — Debt Can Accelerate Growth, but It Creates Fixed Obligations

Debt can be an efficient source of capital when a business has sufficient visibility into future cash flows, allowing promoters to raise capital without immediately diluting ownership. For established businesses, debt may support capacity expansion, asset acquisition, working capital, project development and strategic investments. But debt creates contractual obligations — regardless of whether the business performs above expectations, interest and principal repayment remain due. The central consideration is therefore not simply whether debt is available; it is whether the business can comfortably support the obligations created by that debt, which requires looking beyond the headline borrowing amount to cash-flow generation, existing debt obligations, working capital intensity, future capex, repayment timing and downside resilience.

03 — Equity Provides Flexibility, but Ownership Has Value

Equity capital behaves differently. Unlike traditional debt, it does not generally require fixed periodic repayment, which can provide greater financial flexibility when cash flows are still developing, business outcomes are uncertain, significant upfront investment is required, or growth opportunities require patient capital. However, equity involves ownership — and promoters should not think about dilution only as a percentage of shares issued. The more strategic question is what long-term value is being exchanged for the capital being raised today. A business raising equity during an early growth phase may create substantial value later; in another situation, equity may be used for a requirement that could have been partially supported by debt. Equity should be evaluated against business opportunity, valuation, growth potential, cash-flow profile, alternative financing capacity and long-term promoter objectives.

04 — Internal Accruals Are Valuable but Not Always Sufficient

Retained earnings and internally generated cash provide an important source of capital, reducing interest obligations, external dependence and ownership dilution. However, financing every growth opportunity entirely through internal accruals can also have limitations — a rapidly growing company may face a timing problem, where the business opportunity requires capital today while internal cash generation accumulates only over several years. Waiting may result in lost strategic opportunities. Management therefore often needs to balance capital preservation with capital deployment. The objective is not always to minimise external financing — it is to determine whether external capital can help create value without creating disproportionate financial risk.

The Cost of Capital Is Not Just Financial

Debt Can Cost:

Financial flexibility, future borrowing capacity, security availability and covenant headroom.

Equity Can Cost:

Ownership, decision-making control and future economic participation.

Internal Accruals Can Cost:

Liquidity, speed of growth and the ability to pursue multiple opportunities simultaneously.

This is why capital structure decisions should not be made only through a simple cost comparison. The cheapest visible capital may not always be the most appropriate capital.

05 — Promoters Should Think About the Next Financing Requirement

One common mistake in capital planning is evaluating a transaction in isolation, focusing only on the immediate requirement. But financing decisions create consequences for future transactions — after raising debt today, leverage increases, assets may be encumbered, repayment obligations begin and covenants may apply; after raising equity, ownership changes, future fundraising dynamics change and investor expectations may increase. The current transaction should therefore be considered within a broader capital roadmap, asking what capital requirements this business might have after this transaction — further capacity expansion, working capital increases, a new project or acquisition, and further capital requirements down the line. A financing decision that appears optimal today may reduce flexibility tomorrow.

06 — Growth Can Change the Appropriate Capital Mix

The optimal capital structure for a company is not necessarily static. In the early growth stage, a business may have developing cash flows, higher uncertainty and significant investment requirements. In the established growth stage, it may develop predictable revenues, stronger cash generation, a greater asset base and a longer operating history. At the mature stage, the company may have stable cash flows, established lender relationships, higher internal accruals and greater financing alternatives. The capital mix appropriate at one stage may not be appropriate at another, which is why management should periodically revisit the broader capital structure.

07 — Avoid Funding Long-Term Assets With Short-Term Financial Pressure

One of the most important capital structure considerations is maturity alignment. If capital is invested into an asset that generates value over a long period, the financing structure should consider the timeline over which cash flows will emerge — a mismatch can create avoidable pressure, where a long-term asset with a long implementation and cash-flow cycle, financed with short-term debt, creates repayment pressure and refinancing risk. The problem may not be the investment itself; it may be how it was financed. This is why capital planning should consider both return on investment and cash-flow timing — a profitable investment can still create financial stress if repayment obligations begin before the investment generates adequate cash.

08 — Financial Flexibility Is an Asset

Financial flexibility is difficult to measure on a balance sheet, but it can become extremely valuable when circumstances change. A company with financial flexibility may have the ability to pursue an acquisition, invest in additional capacity, navigate a market slowdown, absorb temporary working capital pressure, or respond to an unexpected opportunity. This flexibility can be influenced by unutilised borrowing capacity, available security, covenant headroom, liquidity reserves and debt maturity profile. A business that maximises leverage during a favourable period may have limited flexibility when the environment changes — the objective should not always be to operate at the highest possible borrowing capacity. Maintaining strategic headroom can have value.

09 — Capital Structure Should Be Tested Against Downside Scenarios

Base-case financial projections can make many capital structures appear manageable. The more useful question is what happens when conditions are less favourable — when revenue growth is slower than expected, margins decline, collections take longer, interest rates increase, project implementation is delayed, or capex requirements increase. The purpose of downside analysis is not to predict failure — it is to understand resilience. A capital structure that works only when every assumption is achieved may leave very little room for execution risk. The objective is to identify how much variation the business can absorb before financial obligations begin creating strategic pressure.

Capital Structure Is a Strategic Decision, Not Only a Treasury Decision

In many companies, financing is treated primarily as a treasury or finance function. But significant capital decisions often have strategic implications — the choice of financing can affect growth speed, ownership, risk appetite, acquisition capacity, dividend policy and strategic independence. Major capital decisions should therefore connect the finance function with broader business strategy, bringing together the business question of what the company is trying to achieve and the capital question of what form and structure of capital best supports that objective. When these conversations occur separately, financing can become reactive. When they are connected, capital becomes part of strategy.

A Practical Framework for Promoters and CFOs

Question 01 — What exactly are we funding?

Define the purpose, amount and timeline.

Question 02 — When will the investment begin generating cash?

Understand the gap between capital deployment and cash generation.

Question 03 — What financial obligations can the business sustainably support?

Look beyond headline profitability. Assess actual cash-flow capacity.

Question 04 — What will this transaction change?

Consider leverage, ownership, security, covenants and future borrowing capacity.

Question 05 — What comes next?

Evaluate the transaction within the broader growth roadmap.

What a Balanced Capital Structure May Look Like

There is no single ideal debt-to-equity ratio that applies to every company. An appropriate capital structure depends on sector, business maturity, cash-flow stability, asset intensity, growth plans and risk profile. The objective is therefore not to achieve a universal ratio — it is to create an appropriate relationship between business risk, capital requirement, cash-flow visibility and financial obligations. The strongest capital structures are often those that are understandable: management knows why the capital was raised, what it is funding, how it will be serviced, what flexibility remains, and what the next capital requirement may be.

EquiFin Perspective

Capital structure is not simply about choosing between debt and equity. It is about deciding how much financial risk, ownership dilution and future obligation a business is willing to accept in order to achieve its growth objectives.

Key Takeaways

  • Growth capital decisions should begin with the purpose of capital rather than the availability of a particular financing product.
  • Debt can preserve ownership but creates contractual financial obligations.
  • Equity can provide patient capital but has a long-term ownership cost.
  • Internal accruals preserve independence but may not always support the speed of growth required.
  • Capital structure decisions should consider future financing requirements, not only the immediate transaction.
  • The appropriate capital mix can change as a business matures and its cash flows become more predictable.
  • Financial flexibility—including borrowing headroom and available security—is strategically valuable.
  • A capital structure should be tested against reasonable downside scenarios rather than only the base case.

Closing Perspective

For promoters, growth is often associated with ambition — a larger facility, a new geography, an acquisition, a major project. Each opportunity creates a question about capital. The answer is not always debt, it is not always equity, and it is rarely only about finding the lowest cost of funds.

The more strategic question is: what capital structure will allow the business to pursue growth while retaining sufficient resilience and flexibility for what comes next? That question becomes increasingly important as companies scale, because capital does more than fund growth — over time, it defines the financial boundaries within which the business will operate. A thoughtful capital structure does not eliminate risk, but it can help ensure that financial decisions remain aligned with the long-term direction of the business.
